Use case: scientific-educational
Asset type: one illustration in a plain-language product-domain tutorial for adults.
Domain: Following the money: how a travel company's financial systems work
Reader: A curious adult from a different field who has never worked in accounting, payments or enterprise software. No knowledge of bookkeeping, SAP or software architecture is assumed.
Scope: An introduction to the financial systems behind a large online travel company: how a booking turns into payments, payouts, revenue, taxes and published financial statements, and why the software that does this needs deliberate design. Booking Holdings (Booking.com, Priceline, Agoda, KAYAK, OpenTable) is the running context, and facts about it come from its public filings and job postings. The guesthouse booking, the people and the amounts in the walkthrough are fictional. Traveler-facing apps, marketing and human-resources systems are outside this guide. Accounting and tax rules differ by country; the explanation describes general patterns for a United States-listed company that operates heavily in Europe.
Subject: The people involved and how they relate
The traveler: Books and pays for a stay, sometimes at booking time and sometimes at the property. Cares about: A clear price, a familiar way to pay (a card, a digital wallet or a local payment method), and a quick, correct refund if plans change.
The property or travel partner: Provides the room, flight, car or table, and is paid by the traveler or by the platform depending on the arrangement. Cares about: Being paid the right amount on time, understanding deductions, and receiving correct commission invoices and tax documents.
The brands and their payments teams: Run the booking sites and apps and the payment services behind them. Booking Holdings describes its financial-services arm as a number of licensed and regulated electronic-money institutions that support the brands. Cares about: Launching new payment methods and markets quickly, keeping payment costs and fraud losses low, and not being blocked by finance.
Finance teams: Controllers, accountants, tax and treasury specialists, and the people who pay partners and suppliers. They own the official numbers. Cares about: Closing the books on time, numbers they can explain line by line, the right tax in every country, and knowing where the company's cash is.
Auditors, regulators and investors: Outside parties who rely on the published numbers. An independent audit firm tests both the numbers and the controls that produce them. Cares about: Evidence that the numbers are complete and accurate, and that the process producing them cannot easily be bypassed or manipulated.
Enterprise architects and engineering teams: Decide how the systems fit together, and build and run them. In a company with several brands, engineers often sit in each brand, while a central architecture team sets shared standards. Cares about: Clear decisions and standards, fewer one-off integrations, systems that can grow with the business, and changes that do not break financial controls.

Teaching scene:
A friendly map of who is involved when a traveler books a guesthouse room through an online travel company. Left: a traveler with a phone. Right: a small guesthouse owner. Center: one large rounded box labeled 'Travel company' containing three smaller boxes labeled 'Payments', 'Finance' and 'Architecture'. Top left: an auditor with a magnifying glass. Top right: a regulator building. Arrows, each with exactly one label: traveler to Payments 'pays'; Payments to guesthouse owner 'payout'; guesthouse owner to Finance, dashed, 'commission invoice (agency)'; Finance up to auditor 'reports and evidence'; regulator down to the Travel company box 'rules'. Architecture has short arrows only to Payments and Finance, labeled 'shared standards'; no arrow connects Architecture to the regulator. Keep labels short and legible; no other text. Do not draw any real company logos or brand marks; use generic icons only. Check: the 'pays' arrow ends at the Payments box (not Finance); the 'reports and evidence' arrow starts at the Finance box (not Payments).

Style and composition:
- Aspect ratio 16:9, generous margins, bright warm-white background.
- A polished adult educational illustration: flat editorial drawing, crisp navy outlines,
  restrained teal, blue, and warm amber accents, subtle texture, very little shading.
- Make the concept understandable at the width of a reading column and on a phone.
- Use a small number of recognizable objects and people. Keep relationships spatially clear.
- Use an inclusive cast without occupational or cultural stereotypes.
- Short everyday labels are welcome where they teach the distinction. Use only labels
  explicitly requested in the teaching scene; otherwise keep the image free of text.
- Arrows must have a clear meaning and direction; do not connect unrelated comparison
  panels as though they are required stages. Do not imply certainty, causality, proportions,
  cash movements, or guaranteed outcomes that the explanation does not support.
- Keep all important content inside the frame. No headline, watermark, brand logo,
  decorative charts, invented statistics, jargon, tiny writing, photorealism, or 3D rendering.
- Explain the idea visually. The surrounding tutorial provides its full definition and caveats.
