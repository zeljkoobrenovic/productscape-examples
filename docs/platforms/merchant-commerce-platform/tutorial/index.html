A beginner's guide

Selling under your own name: how a merchant commerce platform works

About 25 min to readUpdated 2026-10-28

Who this is for

A curious adult coming from a different field who has never run a shop online. No knowledge of retail, payments, or software is assumed.

What this guide covers

An introduction to platforms that let independent merchants run their own branded shop online and in person, using Shopify as the best-known example. It follows one order from a shopper's first visit to the merchant being paid, and covers the platform's role in checkout, payments, stock, shipping, and its ecosystem of add-on apps. Marketplaces where the platform sells to the shopper itself (such as Amazon's retail business), warehouse operations, and detailed tax or customs law are outside this guide. Payment rules, taxes, and import duties differ by country; examples that depend on them are marked as local. The shop, people, and numbers in the walkthrough are fictional.

01

What you will learn

  • Explain why a merchant would rent a commerce platform instead of building a shop or selling only on a marketplace.

  • Distinguish the merchant of record from the platform, and an authorized payment from money actually paid out.

  • Follow one order from discovery to payout, and explain why stock shared between an online shop and a market stall can cause trouble.

  • Describe how the platform earns money and where its interests and the merchant's can pull apart.

  • Explain why fraud, shared stock, and new sales channels such as AI assistants are hard problems rather than simple features.

02

Start with the everyday need

Imagine you make ceramic mugs and want strangers to buy them. You need a place where they can see the mugs, a way to take their money safely, a record of who ordered what, a way to know how many mugs you have left, and a way to get a parcel to their door. A merchant commerce platform rents you all of that as one connected service, under your own shop name and web address, for a monthly fee plus a cut of each sale.

Before such platforms, selling online meant either hiring developers to build and secure a shop, or selling inside someone else's marketplace where the marketplace owns the customer relationship. A platform lets a one-person business look and work like a much larger retailer, and lets a large retailer avoid running its own shop software.

The visible part is the shop page. The larger job is making each promise to a shopper dependable: the price is right, the item exists, the payment is safe, the parcel arrives, and the merchant is paid.

A familiar comparison

Think of a shopping-mall landlord who also provides the shop fittings, the tills, the security guards, and a delivery desk. You rent a unit, put up your own sign, and focus on your products.

Where the comparison stops

A mall landlord brings foot traffic; a commerce platform mostly does not. Shoppers arrive because the merchant attracted them, through social media, search, ads, or word of mouth. The platform is also involved in every payment, so unlike a landlord it sees each sale, takes a share of it, and can hold back money when something looks wrong.

03

The people involved

The merchant

Owns the brand and products, sets prices, and is legally the seller. Ranges from a single founder working from home to a large retailer with hundreds of stores.

What matters to them

Turning visitors into paying customers, keeping fees and fraud losses low, getting paid quickly, and spending less time on admin. Larger merchants also care about connecting to their existing stock and accounting systems.

The shopper

Buys from the merchant, often on a phone, and often having arrived from a social media post, a search result, or an AI assistant's suggestion.

What matters to them

A quick and trustworthy checkout, an accurate delivery promise, easy returns, and not having to type card and address details again.

The platform

Runs the shop software, checkout, and order records for millions of merchants, and usually also processes payments. It is not the seller of the goods.

What matters to them

Merchants growing and staying, because its income rises with their sales. It must also keep fraud and failed payments low across the whole network.

Payment and delivery partners

Card networks, banks, and payment processors move the money; carriers move the parcels. The platform connects to them on the merchant's behalf.

What matters to them

Their own rules and fees. A card network can fine or cut off merchants with too many disputed payments; a carrier charges by weight, distance, and speed.

App developers and agencies

Independent companies that build add-ons (for reviews, subscriptions, loyalty points, and much more) or design and run shops for merchants.

What matters to them

Reaching merchants through the platform's app store and being paid reliably. They depend on the platform not changing its rules or copying their product.

04

The key concepts

Storefront

The pages a shopper sees: the home page, product pages, and cart, shown under the merchant's own name and web address.

For example

A shopper visiting the mug maker's web address sees her logo and photos, not the platform's brand, even though the platform serves every page.

Why it matters

The merchant keeps the customer relationship and the brand. The platform stays mostly in the background, unlike a marketplace where shoppers think of themselves as buying from the marketplace.

Checkout

The steps where a shopper confirms what they are buying, gives a delivery address, sees the final price including shipping and tax, and pays.

For example

A shopper adds two mugs, is shown a shipping charge and sales tax for her address, and pays with a saved card in one tap.

Why it matters

Many shoppers abandon purchases here, so each extra field or surprise cost loses sales. Checkout is also where security rules for card details apply most strictly, which is one reason merchants prefer the platform to run it.

Merchant of record

The business that is legally selling the goods and is responsible for refunds, taxes, and the promise made to the shopper.

For example

When a mug arrives broken, the shopper's claim is against the mug maker, not against the platform that ran the checkout.

Why it matters

It explains who answers for what. The platform provides tools and processes the payment, but the merchant carries the commercial and legal responsibility for the sale.

Authorization, capture, and payout

Authorization is the shopper's bank agreeing that the money is available and setting it aside. Capture is the merchant actually claiming it, usually when the order ships. Payout is the platform sending the collected money, minus fees, to the merchant's bank account some days later.

For example

A card is authorized for 60 dollars on Monday, captured on Tuesday when the parcel ships, and paid out to the merchant on Thursday as part of a batch.

Why it matters

A successful payment on screen is not money in the merchant's bank. Between those moments the sale can still be refunded, disputed, or held for review.

Chargeback

A disputed payment that the shopper's bank reverses, taking the money back from the merchant, often weeks after the sale.

For example

Someone uses a stolen card to buy mugs. The real cardholder disputes the charge; the merchant loses the money and the mugs, and pays a dispute fee.

Why it matters

The person who placed the order is not always the person who pays in the end. Fraud checks before shipping exist to avoid this delayed loss.

Inventory and availability

Inventory is the stock the merchant holds and where it is. Availability is whether a specific item can actually be promised to a specific shopper now.

For example

The merchant has three blue bowls: two in her studio and one on her market stall. Online, only the two in the studio can be shipped tomorrow.

Why it matters

Selling the same last item twice, online and in person, forces a cancellation and an apology. Keeping one shared count across every place a sale can happen is harder than it sounds.

Sales channel

Any place where a shopper can buy: the merchant's own website, a physical till, a social media shop, a marketplace listing, or an AI assistant.

For example

The same mug is listed on the merchant's site, sold from her market stall using a card reader, and shown inside an AI chat when someone asks for a handmade gift.

Why it matters

Each channel has its own rules and costs, but the merchant wants one catalog, one stock count, and one list of orders. Connecting channels is a large part of the platform's value.

App ecosystem

The collection of independent add-ons that merchants install from the platform's app store to extend their shop.

For example

The mug maker installs a reviews app and a gift-wrap app. Each is built by a different company and billed through her platform invoice.

Why it matters

No single platform can build every feature every merchant wants. The ecosystem fills the gaps, but it also means a merchant's shop depends on several companies at once.

05

A little history

These turning points explain present practices, with Shopify used as the example platform. Other platforms followed different paths, and earlier hosted shop builders existed before it.

  1. Late 1800s to 1990s

    Mail-order catalogs, then telephone ordering, let sellers reach distant buyers without a shop in every town. Merchants still had to handle payment, stock, and shipping themselves.

    What this helps explain

    The basic chain of a remote sale, from promise to payment to delivery, is old. Online selling changed who could afford to run it, not what had to be done.

  2. Mid-1990s to early 2000s

    Web shops appeared, but most were custom-built or assembled from software the merchant had to host and secure. Large marketplaces offered an easier route at the cost of owning the customer relationship.

    What this helps explain

    This set up the choice that remains today: build it yourself, sell inside a marketplace, or rent a platform that sits in between.

  3. December 2004

    The major card brands jointly published the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS), a common set of security rules for anyone storing, processing, or transmitting card details.

    What this helps explain

    Handling card data safely became a demanding compliance job. That made it more attractive for small merchants to let a platform run checkout and carry most of that burden.

    Source for December 2004

  4. 2004 to 2009

    Shopify's founders built software to sell snowboards online, found the software more useful than the shop, and launched it as a hosted platform in 2006. In 2009 they opened it to outside developers through an app store.

    What this helps explain

    The model of renting shop software by the month, with independent developers extending it, became the template for many platforms that followed.

    Source for 2004 to 2009

  5. 2010s

    Platforms added their own payment processing, card readers for physical shops, saved-wallet checkouts, and versions for large retailers. Shopping moved heavily to phones.

    What this helps explain

    The platform shifted from selling software to taking part in every transaction. Payments became a major source of its income and gave it a view of fraud across many merchants.

  6. August 2025 (United States, local)

    The United States ended the duty-free exemption for low-value imported parcels from all countries.

    What this helps explain

    Merchants shipping small orders into the US suddenly faced duties and customs paperwork on parcels that used to pass freely. It shows how a rule outside the platform's control can change a merchant's costs overnight.

    Source for August 2025 (United States, local)

  7. 2025 to 2026

    AI assistants began recommending and buying products inside a conversation. In January 2026 Google and Shopify announced the Universal Commerce Protocol, an open standard for letting AI systems check out from merchants; a rival protocol from OpenAI and Stripe also exists.

    What this helps explain

    A new sales channel appeared where the shopper may never visit the merchant's site. Platforms must give assistants accurate products, prices, and stock while keeping the merchant as the seller.

    Source for 2025 to 2026

06

Follow one complete example

Two mugs, a market stall, and the last blue bowl (fictional)

Lena runs a one-person ceramics brand from her studio. She uses a commerce platform for her website, takes card payments at a weekend market with the platform's card reader, and has installed a reviews app. Sam, a shopper in another city, sees a video of her blue bowls on social media. Lena, Sam, and all amounts are invented for illustration.

  1. Sam arrives at the storefront

    Sam taps the link in the video and lands on Lena's product page. The page shows photos, the price, reviews from earlier buyers, and that one blue bowl is left. The platform served the page quickly on a phone and the reviews app supplied the ratings.

    Why this step matters

    Lena paid for the visit with her own marketing. The platform's job is not to wreck that effort with a slow page or a confusing layout.

  2. Sam goes to checkout

    Sam adds the blue bowl and two mugs. At checkout the platform recognizes Sam's phone number from a saved wallet used at another shop on the same platform, fills in the address and card, and shows shipping and sales tax for that address. Total: 92 dollars.

    Why this step matters

    Sam never typed a card number, which raises the chance the sale completes. Tax and shipping are calculated by the platform, but Lena, as merchant of record, is still responsible for them being right.

    When things go differently

    A surprise shipping cost at this step is one of the most common reasons shoppers give up.

  3. The payment is authorized and checked

    Sam's bank authorizes 92 dollars. In parallel, the platform's fraud checks compare the order with patterns seen across many merchants: is this card, device, and address combination normal? The order is scored low-risk and marked paid.

    Why this step matters

    Authorization reserves Sam's money; it has not reached Lena. The fraud check protects Lena from a chargeback weeks later, which a single small merchant could not judge well from her own few orders.

  4. The exception: the last bowl sells twice

    At the same moment, Lena's friend is minding the market stall, where the one remaining blue bowl is on display. A visitor buys it with the card reader. Because the stall and the website share one stock count, whichever sale is recorded first claims the bowl. The website sale was recorded a few seconds earlier, so the reader warns that the bowl is sold, and Lena's friend apologizes and offers a different bowl.

    Why this step matters

    Without a shared count, both sales would succeed and one customer would get a cancellation days later. Shared stock across channels is valuable precisely because these collisions happen.

    When things go differently

    Shared counting only works if every channel records sales promptly. A till working offline with a bad connection can still oversell, and someone must decide which customer to disappoint.

  5. Lena packs and ships

    Lena sees the order in her admin screen, buys a discounted shipping label through the platform, and prints it. Marking the order shipped captures the payment and sends Sam a tracking link.

    Why this step matters

    Capture happens at shipping so Sam is not charged for something that never leaves. The carrier now holds the delivery promise, but Sam will still blame Lena if it is late.

  6. Lena is paid

    Two days later the platform pays out Lena's week of sales in one transfer. From Sam's 92 dollars it has subtracted a payment processing fee, and the shipping label cost was billed separately. Lena's monthly subscription and her reviews app appear on a separate platform bill.

    Why this step matters

    Lena's real margin depends on all these deductions together. Payout timing also matters: a small business may need this week's sales to buy next week's clay.

The outcome

Sam receives the bowl and mugs, leaves a review that will help the next shopper decide, and is now in Lena's customer list for future emails. Lena earned a sale she could not have handled alone, and avoided a double sale. The platform earned fees from the subscription, the payment, and the shipping label, and its fraud checks learned from one more normal order. Success for everyone means the same thing: the promise made on the product page was kept.

07

Value, money, and incentives

Who pays, and for what

Merchants pay a monthly subscription for the software, a percentage plus a small fixed amount on each card payment, and optional extras such as shipping labels, loans, or premium support. App developers pay the platform a share of what merchants pay them. Shoppers usually pay nothing directly to the platform.

For example

For Lena, the subscription is a fixed cost; the payment fee grows with every sale. In Shopify's case, the merchant services tied to sales, payments above all, bring in most of its revenue, not the subscriptions.

Aligned interests: the platform grows when merchants sell more

Because most income scales with sales, the platform benefits from faster checkouts, better fraud decisions, and new channels that bring merchants more customers.

For example

A saved wallet that lets Sam pay in one tap helps Lena sell, helps the platform earn a fee, and saves Sam time.

Where interests pull apart

The platform may favor its own payment service, apps, or features over alternatives a merchant would prefer. It may hold back payouts when fraud risk rises, which protects the network but can starve a small business of cash. App developers fear that the platform will build a feature that replaces their app.

For example

A merchant using an outside payment provider may pay an extra platform fee on each sale, a setting that steers merchants toward the platform's own payments.

Network effects across merchants

Some benefits come from the platform serving many merchants at once: a shopper's saved wallet works at every shop, fraud signals are learned from millions of orders, and shipping rates are negotiated in bulk.

For example

Lena got a lower shipping price than she could have negotiated alone, and Sam's wallet worked on her shop the first time Sam visited.

Signs of success

Useful signs include how many visitors become buyers, how many merchants stay and grow from year to year, the share of sales paid through the platform's own payments, fraud and chargeback rates, and how quickly merchants are paid. Platforms also watch total sales across all merchants, often called gross merchandise volume, because their income follows it.

For example

If merchants leave after their first year, a platform with a growing sign-up count can still be shrinking in the way that matters.

08

What makes this domain difficult

Stopping fraud without blocking good shoppers

Fraudsters test stolen cards on small shops, and chargebacks arrive weeks after the parcel has gone. The platform must decide in a fraction of a second, on limited information, whether to accept an order.

The competing needs

Stricter checks reduce losses but also reject real customers, who rarely retry. Looser checks win sales but leave merchants with chargebacks and fees.

In practice

A first-time buyer with a new phone, a gift address, and an expensive order looks like fraud and also like a genuine holiday shopper.

How people respond, and its limits

Score each order using patterns across all merchants, flag uncertain ones for the merchant to review, and offer protection where the platform covers approved orders that later turn out fraudulent. The limits: shared scoring can treat unusual but honest businesses unfairly, and the merchant may not understand why an order was blocked.

One stock count across many channels

The same item can be sold on the website, at a till, in a social media shop, on a marketplace, and through an AI assistant, each recording sales at slightly different times and some working offline.

The competing needs

Holding stock back for each channel avoids overselling but leaves items unsold in one place while another runs out. Pooling it sells more but risks selling the same item twice.

In practice

Lena's last blue bowl: the shared count saved one customer from a cancellation, but only because both sales were recorded within seconds.

How people respond, and its limits

Keep a central count, reserve stock at checkout, and let merchants set safety buffers per channel. The limits: buffers waste stock, and connections to outside channels may update only every few minutes.

Serving a founder and a large retailer with the same product

A new merchant needs things simple and cheap. A large retailer needs custom workflows, links to its existing systems, many countries, and wholesale prices for business buyers.

The competing needs

Every option added for large merchants can make the product more confusing for small ones. Building separately for each risks two products that drift apart.

In practice

A wholesale price list that hides retail prices from business buyers is essential to a large retailer and irrelevant to Lena.

How people respond, and its limits

Keep one core platform with tiered plans and let apps and agencies supply specialized needs. The limits: large merchants may still outgrow it, and many installed apps can slow shops and complicate support.

Selling through AI assistants

When an assistant recommends and buys a product inside a conversation, the shopper may never see the merchant's site. The assistant needs accurate prices, stock, shipping, and return terms, and the merchant must still be the seller responsible for the sale.

The competing needs

Opening products to assistants can bring new buyers, but the merchant gives up some control over presentation and the relationship, and competing protocols mean extra work to be present everywhere.

In practice

Someone asks an assistant for a handmade blue bowl under 50 dollars. If the assistant shows Lena's bowl after it has already sold, the shopper is let down and may blame Lena.

How people respond, and its limits

Publish a structured, frequently updated product catalog, support open checkout protocols, and keep the payment and order recorded in the merchant's own system. The limits: standards are new and still changing, and the assistant decides which products to show.

Rules that change from outside

Taxes, import duties, card network rules, and privacy laws differ by country and can change quickly. Merchants rely on the platform to keep up, but they remain legally responsible.

The competing needs

Automating tax and duty calculations helps small merchants, but every country added is ongoing work and an exposure if it is wrong.

In practice

When the United States ended duty-free entry for low-value parcels in 2025, merchants abroad had to decide whether to collect duties at checkout or let shoppers face surprise charges at delivery.

How people respond, and its limits

Build tax and duty calculation into checkout and update rules centrally. The limits: the merchant must still register, file, and answer to authorities, and coverage for smaller countries can lag.

09

Common misunderstandings

The platform sells the products, so it handles complaints.

A better way to understand it

The merchant is the seller of record. The platform provides tools and usually processes the payment, but the shopper's claim is against the merchant.

Having an online shop brings customers.

A better way to understand it

A platform mostly does not bring shoppers to a merchant. Merchants usually attract visitors themselves through social media, search, ads, or word of mouth; the platform turns those visitors into orders.

Once the payment succeeds, the money is the merchant's.

A better way to understand it

An approved payment can still be refunded, disputed as a chargeback, or held for review. Money reaches the merchant's bank only at payout, minus fees.

Stock is just a number you type in.

A better way to understand it

Stock exists in places and is promised across several channels at once. Keeping that promise accurate when sales happen simultaneously is a real coordination problem.

10

The ideas to take with you

  • A merchant commerce platform rents merchants the whole chain of selling (storefront, checkout, payments, stock, shipping, and add-ons) under the merchant's own brand.

  • The merchant remains the seller and carries responsibility; the platform is the infrastructure and is paid mainly in proportion to sales.

  • A sale is a series of promises: the item exists, the price is right, the payment is genuine, the parcel arrives. Most difficulty lies in keeping those promises at the moments they are tested.

  • Payment is not one moment: authorization, capture, payout, and possible chargeback happen at different times, with risk between them.

  • New channels such as AI assistants change where shoppers buy, but not who is responsible for the sale.

11

Try it for yourself

Pause and think through each situation, then open the answer.

1. Sam's bowl arrives cracked. Who should Sam ask for a refund, and why?

Reveal answer to question 1

Lena, the merchant.

The reasoning

Lena is the merchant of record. The platform ran the checkout and processed the payment, but it did not sell the bowl, so the commercial promise and the refund obligation are Lena's.

2. Lena's admin screen shows a paid order on Monday. Why might she still not have that money on Friday?

Reveal answer to question 2

Paid on screen means authorized or captured, not paid out.

The reasoning

The money reaches her bank only in a later payout, minus fees. In between, the order could be refunded, disputed as a chargeback, or held if fraud risk looks high.

3. Why did the market stall's card reader refuse to sell the last blue bowl, and what would have happened without that?

Reveal answer to question 3

The website and the stall shared one stock count, and the website sale was recorded first.

The reasoning

Without a shared count both sales would have succeeded, and one customer would later have received a cancellation. Sharing stock across channels prevents that, but only if each channel records sales promptly.

4. A platform makes most of its money from fees on each sale rather than subscriptions. Name one way this helps merchants and one way it can work against them.

Reveal answer to question 4

It rewards the platform for helping merchants sell more, but it can also push the platform to steer merchants toward its own paid services.

The reasoning

Faster checkout and better fraud decisions raise both the merchant's sales and the platform's fees. The same incentive can lead to extra fees for using an outside payment provider, or holding payouts to protect the network at a small merchant's expense.

5. An AI assistant recommends Lena's bowl after it has sold out. What does this tell you about selling through assistants?

Reveal answer to question 5

The assistant needs accurate, frequently updated stock and price information from the merchant's system.

The reasoning

The shopper never visits Lena's site, so the assistant's information is the only promise they see. Stale data creates a broken promise that the shopper will attribute to Lena, the seller.

12

Questions to ask a practitioner

  • Of all your sales, how much is lost to fraud and chargebacks, and how much do your fraud checks block by mistake?

  • How long after a sale does money actually reach your bank, and has a payout ever been held?

  • Which channels do you sell through, and how often have you sold an item you did not have?

  • Which apps would break your business if they disappeared tomorrow?

  • What share of your sales now starts from social media, search, or an AI assistant rather than people typing your address?
