A beginner's guide

From a travel idea to a trip that works

Who this is for

Someone arriving from another field, with no assumed knowledge of travel businesses, accounting, or business software.

What this guide covers

An introduction to the work of tour operators and the software that connects planning, sales, suppliers, travellers, and finance. Examples reflect group and tailor-made travel in a European setting. Legal duties depend on the arrangement, country, and applicable dates. This is not a guide to running an airline or choosing a holiday. Nora, Coast & Village Travel, the trip, and all prices and contract terms are fictional.

02

Start with the everyday need

Imagine arranging a weekend away for friends. The dates must work, beds must be available, transport must arrive, and everyone needs the right instructions. A tour operator does this professionally, often coordinating services supplied by several other businesses. Some sell scheduled group trips; others build a different itinerary for each customer.

The visible product is a holiday description and a booking button. Behind it are promises, supplier requests, traveller details, bills, and decisions about changes. Tour operator ERP software connects this work. ERP means enterprise resource planning: here, shared business records and workflows linking the trip being sold to the services and money behind it.

A familiar comparison

Think of organizing a wedding using an independent venue, caterer, photographer, and transport company. Each supplier can do its own job well, yet the event still needs someone to coordinate the whole.

Where the comparison stops

Travel businesses may coordinate many departures at once, sell through other agents, and support people far from home. Package-travel rules can also place responsibilities on the organizer. The software provider supplies tools; the travel business makes and manages the travel arrangements.

03

The people involved

Travellers

Choose and pay for a trip, provide needed details, and experience the result. The person booking may be arranging travel for several people.

What matters to them

An accurate description, a workable trip, clear terms, and timely help when something changes.

The tour operator

Designs trips, agrees supplier terms, sells places, coordinates delivery, and manages the finances. Nora is a trip planner at fictional Coast & Village Travel.

What matters to them

Keeping promises while covering costs. Sales, operations, service, and finance colleagues need compatible versions of the same information.

Suppliers and destination partners

Provide accommodation, transport, guiding, or activities. A destination management company is a local business that can coordinate several such services for the operator.

What matters to them

Clear requests, realistic timings, confirmed quantities, and payment under the agreed terms.

Travel agents and other resellers

Help customers choose and book travel supplied by another business. Their exact commercial and legal role depends on the arrangement.

What matters to them

Reliable availability, clear booking status, and any commission: a fee earned for selling the trip.

The software provider

Supplies and supports the business system, websites, or portals. The travel organization pays for access and often for setup or additional services.

What matters to them

Reliable workflows, usable connections to other systems, and customers who can operate the software confidently. It does not own the hotel rooms or deliver the holiday.

04

The key concepts

Travel components and packages

A component is one travel service, such as a hotel stay or a guided walk. An operator combines components into an offer. A package has a specific legal meaning; under EU rules, certain combinations of different travel-service types qualify, depending on how they are offered and booked.

For example

Nora combines minibus travel, two nights at a hotel, and a guided walk. She needs to know both what the customer is buying and which business must arrange each part.

Why it matters

Customers experience the connected trip. A lovely hotel does not solve a missing transfer. Legal classification also affects the organizer's obligations.

A travel product and a departure

The product describes the trip being offered. A departure is a particular run of that trip on particular dates. Each departure has its own capacity, bookings, supplier arrangements, and outcome.

For example

Coast & Village Weekend is the product. Its September and October departures may follow the same route but use different guides or have different numbers of travellers.

Why it matters

A good description can be reused; availability cannot simply be copied from one departure to another. A tailor-made trip may instead have one customer-specific set of dates and services.

Availability, allotments, and release dates

Availability means the required services can be supplied for the right dates and people. An allotment is a quantity a supplier makes available under an agreement. A release date is an agreed point when unused capacity may return to the supplier. Payment and cancellation commitments depend on that agreement.

For example

Nora needs three twin rooms on each of two nights for six travellers who agree to share. Six minibus passenger places do not help if only two suitable rooms are available on the second night.

Why it matters

Rooms, beds, seats, dates, and room-sharing arrangements are different things. Holding capacity can improve certainty while creating deadlines or financial exposure.

Quote, booking, and supplier confirmation

A quote describes an offer and its terms. A booking records the customer's arrangement with the seller. Supplier confirmation records a supplier's agreement to provide a particular service. Systems track both the overall booking and the individual services; the labels and contract effects depend on the business process.

For example

A traveller accepts Nora's offer, while the hotel request is still awaiting a reply. That customer action does not itself make the hotel rooms confirmed.

Why it matters

An overall status can hide an unresolved component. The team must communicate accurately and avoid promises that the supplier arrangements cannot support.

The itinerary and operational lists

An itinerary tells travellers what happens when and where. Operational lists tell the people delivering the trip what they need to do: for example, a rooming list assigns guests to room arrangements, while a pickup list helps a driver collect the right people.

For example

Travellers need the hotel address and meeting time. The hotel needs agreed room arrangements. The guide needs the group schedule and relevant requirements.

Why it matters

Different people need different views of consistent information. Sharing every traveller detail with everyone creates confusion and unnecessary privacy exposure.

Sales, direct costs, and trip margin

Sales are the amounts charged to customers for the trip. Direct costs are costs assigned to delivering it, such as the hotel, transport, and guide. In this guide, trip margin means sales minus those direct costs. It still has to help cover office salaries, software, marketing, and other costs.

For example

Six travellers paying €150 produce €900 of sales. The minibus costs €300, hotel €300, and guide €100: €700 in direct costs. The trip margin is €200. All figures are illustrative and exclude taxes and other costs.

Why it matters

A busy departure is not automatically profitable. Some costs stay much the same when fewer people travel; others change with the number of people or rooms.

Cash timing and settlement

Cash timing concerns when money actually arrives and leaves. A deposit is an advance payment, not necessarily the whole price. Settlement means checking and completing amounts owed. Reconciliation means matching records, such as a bank receipt to the booking it pays for.

For example

The six travellers pay €45 deposits, bringing in €270. Suppose supplier advances of €350 are due before the remaining customer payments. The operator must plan the €80 timing gap, even though the trip is expected to have a positive margin.

Why it matters

Expected margin does not pay a bill today. Finance must also follow applicable rules for protecting customer money and handle refunds or disputed amounts correctly.

05

A little history

These British, American, and EU examples explain parts of today's travel business. They are selected milestones, not a claim that one company invented organized travel or that all countries developed in the same way.

  1. 1841: organizing a shared journey

    Thomas Cook organized a rail excursion from Leicester to Loughborough. It is a well-known example of arranging travel for a group before modern booking systems.

    What this helps explain

    The coordination job is older than the software: connecting people, transport, timing, and an understandable offer.

    Source for 1841: organizing a shared journey

  2. The 1960s: shared reservation records

    American Airlines and IBM's Sabre system connected reservation offices to centralized flight information and booking processing.

    What this helps explain

    This airline example shows why shared availability and reservation records matter when many people sell limited capacity. A tour still requires coordination beyond an airline reservation.

    Source for The 1960s: shared reservation records

  3. 2015 onward: rules adapt to assembled travel

    The EU's 2015 Package Travel Directive addressed both pre-arranged holidays and certain trips assembled through newer booking processes. It was amended in 2026.

    What this helps explain

    How a trip is offered and bought can affect responsibilities. Teams need to check the rules and application dates relevant to their arrangements.

    Source for 2015 onward: rules adapt to assembled travel

06

Follow one complete example

Nora organizes a six-person weekend

This fictional three-day trip includes minibus travel, two hotel nights, and a guided walk. Six adults agree to share three twin rooms. The €150 price per person and all supplier amounts are teaching figures, not real travel prices. Assume taxes and other costs are excluded throughout the example.

  1. Describe the trip and who it suits

    Nora records the route, dates, inclusions, room arrangements, meeting points, and practical requirements. She makes the offer clear enough for travellers to judge whether it suits them.

    Why this step matters

    Good preparation starts with a trip people can actually take, including any relevant access or mobility requirements.

  2. Arrange supply and calculate the offer

    She checks suitable transport, three twin rooms for both nights, and a guide. She records supplier terms and deadlines. Expected direct costs are €700 against €900 sales if all six places sell.

    Why this step matters

    Capacity, price, and commitments must fit the same departure. Nora also considers the effect of selling fewer places.

  3. Record bookings and confirm each component

    The six travellers accept the offer. Nora records their arrangements and supplier replies, resolving pending items before treating the trip as secured. Finance matches €270 of customer deposits to bookings and plans the €350 of supplier advances.

    Why this step matters

    Customer commitments, supplier commitments, and payments remain visible as distinct facts.

  4. Prepare the people delivering the trip

    Nora checks guest details and agreed room sharing, then prepares itineraries, rooming information, pickup details, and guide instructions. Each recipient gets the information needed for their role.

    Why this step matters

    A confirmed hotel still needs to know whom to expect. Preparing different documents from shared records reduces contradictions.

  5. Handle a hotel problem

    The hotel can no longer provide the rooms. Nora finds a suitable alternative, explains the change and available options, and checks the applicable obligations. In this case all travellers accept, and the operator keeps their price unchanged.

    Why this step matters

    Assume the original hotel payment is fully refunded and the replacement costs €360. Direct costs rise to €760, leaving €140 trip margin.

    When things go differently

    Nora updates the hotel details, rooming instructions, driver destination, guide plan, customer itinerary, and financial records. Changing only the customer email would leave other people working from the old plan.

  6. Deliver, check, and close the trip

    The group travels and the team handles any questions. Afterwards, finance matches final supplier bills, refunds, and customer payments to the records. Nora reviews traveller feedback and the departure's actual costs.

    Why this step matters

    The group returning home and the accounts being settled are separate milestones. Actual results inform the next departure.

The outcome

The travellers complete the agreed trip with the replacement hotel. Everyone delivering it receives the updated plan, and the operator can explain the €140 margin before other costs. The software supports these handoffs; Nora and her colleagues make and check the decisions.

07

Value, money, and incentives

The traveller buys coordination

The benefit is a useful whole: a suitable trip, manageable arrangements, and a contact when something changes. Combining services adds work as well as convenience.

For example

Nora's hotel replacement protects the trip because she also changes transport and joining instructions.

The travel business and software business earn differently

The operator receives travel sales and pays suppliers and operating costs. An agent may earn commission. The software provider receives fees for the system, implementation, support, or additional services.

For example

The €900 trip sale is the operator's transaction. It is not €900 of revenue for its software supplier.

Reliable operations need resources

Supplier relationships, knowledgeable staff, accurate records, and disruption handling cost time and money. Faster quoting is useful when the resulting offer remains deliverable.

For example

A sales team rewarded only for booking volume may overlook expensive changes that appear later in the departure's accounts.

08

What makes this domain difficult

Promising capacity that several businesses control

A supplier can change availability while requests move between people and systems. The trip needs its components to fit together.

The competing needs

Holding more capacity improves certainty but may create commitments or release deadlines.

In practice

Six seats are available while a hotel has too few rooms for the agreed sharing arrangements.

How people respond, and its limits

Track service-level confirmation and deadlines, check important replies, and keep useful alternatives. An online connection helps only when its data and booking responses are reliable.

Making each trip personal without losing control

A room change, access requirement, or extra activity can affect prices, capacity, documents, and supplier instructions.

The competing needs

More choice helps customers but increases the combinations the team must check.

In practice

A traveller requests a room alone after the original plan assumed sharing.

How people respond, and its limits

Record the request once, examine its effects, and confirm the agreed change with the affected parties. A prettier itinerary cannot resolve an unavailable room.

Keeping departures financially viable

Some supplier costs remain even when fewer people book. Seasonality and advance payments can add pressure.

The competing needs

An attractive price can increase demand while leaving less room to handle disruption.

In practice

The replacement hotel reduces Nora's expected €200 margin to €140.

How people respond, and its limits

Review costs at different group sizes and update forecasts as commitments change. Track cash dates separately; neither a sales total nor an expected margin gives the whole picture.

Helping travellers during a disruption

Time is short, several parties may be affected, and the operator must understand its obligations as well as the practical alternatives.

The competing needs

A quick fix may cost more; a slow decision can leave people uncertain or stranded.

In practice

A replacement hotel is useful only if it fits the travellers' needs and everyone receives the new instructions.

How people respond, and its limits

Assign a decision-maker, keep contact and commitment records accessible, and communicate clear options. Automation can distribute an update, but cannot judge every alternative's suitability.

Sharing accurate information with the right people

Travellers, agents, hotels, and guides need different details. Old versions and unnecessary personal data can spread easily.

The competing needs

Easy access supports delivery; indiscriminate sharing creates confusion and privacy exposure.

In practice

A driver needs pickup information, not the operator's complete customer file.

How people respond, and its limits

Use role-appropriate views, version important documents, and review sensitive requests. A shared system helps only if people maintain the records and use the current version.

09

Common misunderstandings

A booking button means every component is confirmed.

A better way to understand it

The process may still contain supplier requests or conditions. Check the actual arrangement and individual service statuses.

A room, a bed, and a place on the trip are interchangeable.

A better way to understand it

Capacity must fit dates, people, and agreed arrangements. One available room does not automatically satisfy any two travellers.

Customer deposits are profit.

A better way to understand it

They are cash received towards a booking. Supplier obligations, remaining payments, refunds, and other costs still need attention.

The software organizes and delivers the holiday by itself.

A better way to understand it

It connects records and supports repeatable work. People and suppliers still arrange, deliver, and resolve the trip.

10

The ideas to take with you

  • The operator coordinates a whole trip from services delivered by several parties.

  • Products, departures, customer bookings, and supplier confirmations describe different things.

  • Changing one component can change many people's instructions and the trip's economics.

  • Successful delivery, financial settlement, and useful feedback all matter after the sale.

11

Try it for yourself

Pause and think through each situation, then open the answer.

1. A customer accepts the offer and the minibus is confirmed, but the hotel has not replied. Is the complete trip secured?

Reveal answer to question 1

No. The hotel remains an unresolved component that must be handled and communicated accurately.

The reasoning

The customer's booking and each supplier's commitment are separate facts.

2. There are six passenger places but only two twin rooms available on the second night. Can Nora promise the planned arrangements for all six travellers?

Reveal answer to question 2

No. The plan needs three twin rooms on each night, assuming the agreed sharing arrangement.

The reasoning

Availability must work across all required services and dates; sufficient transport alone does not settle it.

3. The trip sells for €900 and the replacement raises direct costs to €760. Is the remaining €140 the business's final profit?

Reveal answer to question 3

No. It is the trip margin as defined here, before the other costs and taxes excluded from this example.

The reasoning

Office staff, software, marketing, and other expenses still have to be covered.

4. Customer deposits of €270 have arrived and €350 of supplier advances are due. What does finance need to notice?

Reveal answer to question 4

An €80 timing gap, along with any rules governing how customer money is held and used.

The reasoning

A positive expected trip margin does not mean enough cash is available at every payment date.

Questions to ask a practitioner

  • Which supplier confirmations are hardest to obtain or keep current?

  • What changes most often between a quote and the final trip?

  • Who checks the effects of a change on travellers, suppliers, documents, and money?

  • How do you know a departure is both operationally finished and financially settled?
