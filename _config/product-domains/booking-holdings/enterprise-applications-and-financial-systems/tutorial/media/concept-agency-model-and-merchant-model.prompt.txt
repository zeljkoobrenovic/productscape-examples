Use case: scientific-educational
Asset type: one illustration in a plain-language product-domain tutorial for adults.
Domain: Following the money: how a travel company's financial systems work
Reader: A curious adult from a different field who has never worked in accounting, payments or enterprise software. No knowledge of bookkeeping, SAP or software architecture is assumed.
Scope: An introduction to the financial systems behind a large online travel company: how a booking turns into payments, payouts, revenue, taxes and published financial statements, and why the software that does this needs deliberate design. Booking Holdings (Booking.com, Priceline, Agoda, KAYAK, OpenTable) is the running context, and facts about it come from its public filings and job postings. The guesthouse booking, the people and the amounts in the walkthrough are fictional. Traveler-facing apps, marketing and human-resources systems are outside this guide. Accounting and tax rules differ by country; the explanation describes general patterns for a United States-listed company that operates heavily in Europe.
Subject: Agency model and merchant model
Meaning: In the agency model the traveler pays the property directly, usually at the stay, and the travel company later invoices the property for its commission. In the merchant model the travel company takes the traveler's payment itself, holds it, and pays the property after deducting its share.
Concrete example: Under agency, a guesthouse charges the guest 300 euros at checkout and later receives an invoice for 45 euros of commission. Under merchant, the platform collects the 300 euros at booking and later pays the guesthouse 255 euros.
Why this matters: The two models create completely different financial work. Agency means invoicing partners and collecting from them. Merchant means handling travelers' payments, refunds and disputes, holding money and paying partners. Booking Holdings reports that 70 percent of its gross bookings were on a merchant basis in 2025, up from 63 percent in 2024, so the merchant workload is growing fast.

Teaching scene:
Side-by-side comparison in two panels labeled 'Agency' and 'Merchant'. Three characters appear in each panel: a traveler with a backpack, a small guesthouse with its owner, and a generic travel platform shown as a plain laptop icon labeled 'Platform'. Agency panel: one arrow from the traveler to the guesthouse labeled '300', and one arrow from the guesthouse owner to the Platform labeled '45 commission'. The traveler has no arrow to the Platform. Merchant panel: one arrow from the traveler to the Platform labeled '300', and one arrow from the Platform to the guesthouse owner labeled '255'. The guesthouse sends nothing to the traveler. Arrowheads must clearly point in these directions. Only these labels. Do not draw any real company logos or brand marks; use generic icons only.

Style and composition:
- Aspect ratio 16:9, generous margins, bright warm-white background.
- A polished adult educational illustration: flat editorial drawing, crisp navy outlines,
  restrained teal, blue, and warm amber accents, subtle texture, very little shading.
- Make the concept understandable at the width of a reading column and on a phone.
- Use a small number of recognizable objects and people. Keep relationships spatially clear.
- Use an inclusive cast without occupational or cultural stereotypes.
- Short everyday labels are welcome where they teach the distinction. Use only labels
  explicitly requested in the teaching scene; otherwise keep the image free of text.
- Arrows must have a clear meaning and direction; do not connect unrelated comparison
  panels as though they are required stages. Do not imply certainty, causality, proportions,
  cash movements, or guaranteed outcomes that the explanation does not support.
- Keep all important content inside the frame. No headline, watermark, brand logo,
  decorative charts, invented statistics, jargon, tiny writing, photorealism, or 3D rendering.
- Explain the idea visually. The surrounding tutorial provides its full definition and caveats.
