A beginner's guide

Keeping property, people, money, and maintenance connected

About 18 min to readUpdated 2026-09-09

Who this is for

A curious adult from another field. No knowledge of property management, accounting, tenancy law, or business software is assumed.

What this guide covers

An introduction to the ongoing management of property and the software supporting it. The recurring example is a Dutch apartment building with rental homes and an owners' association. Commercial leases and other countries, including Germany, have different rules. Buying an investment, mortgage advice, and detailed legal procedures are outside this guide. Canal Court, its people, contracts, prices, and financial examples are fictional.

01

What you will learn

  • Explain how property management connects a physical place, its users, agreements, money, and upkeep.

  • Distinguish ownership from tenancy and management, and an apartment from shared building parts.

  • Follow rent administration and a repair through the people and records involved.

  • Explain why a payment, an invoice, a completed job, and a useful report are different things.

02

Start with the everyday need

Imagine renting a flat. You receive keys, pay agreed charges, and contact someone when the roof leaks. Behind those ordinary moments, someone must know which home you rent, who owns it, what the agreement says, where payments belong, and who can authorize repairs. Property management keeps these arrangements working over time.

A property business may manage many buildings for different owners. A wrong link can send a bill to the wrong person or leave a repair waiting with the wrong team. Real estate ERP software connects the records and workflows. ERP means enterprise resource planning: here, shared administration for properties, contracts, finances, maintenance, and the people using them.

A familiar comparison

Think of running a community center with several rooms, regular users, shared heating, and visiting repair companies. A room booking calendar is useful, but it cannot answer every question about agreements, bills, or the leaking roof.

Where the comparison stops

Property arrangements may last years, ownership can change, and tenants have rights that go beyond a booking. A manager can administer property without owning it. The software company provides tools; it does not become the landlord or perform the repair.

03

The people involved

The tenant or occupier

Uses the premises under an agreement and communicates about payments, access, and problems. Sam rents apartment 2 at fictional Canal Court.

What matters to them

A usable home, understandable charges, fair treatment, privacy, and clear responses when something goes wrong.

The owner or investor

Owns the property interest and makes decisions within the applicable rules. Ari owns and lets apartment 2.

What matters to them

Reliable income, the condition of the property, costs, and responsibilities. An asset manager may help an investor make decisions across several properties.

The property manager

Handles agreed administration and coordination for an owner. Mira manages Ari's rental; her authority comes from the management arrangement.

What matters to them

Clear responsibilities, accurate records, prompt service, and fees that cover the work. She needs to know which decisions she can make and which require approval.

The owners' association

Coordinates shared interests in an apartment building. In the Netherlands it is called a vereniging van eigenaars, or VvE. Apartment owners are its members.

What matters to them

Shared maintenance, contributions, reserves, and properly recorded decisions. It is distinct from an individual landlord and from a firm hired to administer it.

Contractors and other suppliers

Inspect, repair, clean, or maintain property under an agreed scope. They need access and someone authorized to instruct the work.

What matters to them

A clear problem description, safe access, timely decisions, and payment for work that has been properly agreed and checked.

The software provider

Supplies the system and services such as setup, migration, training, and support. Owners or management organizations pay for its use.

What matters to them

Dependable records and workflows that customers can use effectively. Property rent and the provider's software fees are different revenue streams.

04

The key concepts

Portfolio, building, and unit

A portfolio is a collection of properties managed or considered together. A building is a physical structure. A unit is a separately identified space, such as an apartment, shop, or parking place. Shared parts, such as a roof or stairwell, may need their own records and responsibilities.

For example

Canal Court contains four apartments. Sam lives in unit 2, but the roof serves the building. Ari's ownership, Sam's tenancy, and Mira's management role must be linked to the right place and period.

Why it matters

An address alone may be too broad to identify a contract or cost. The place where a problem appears is not always the part that needs repairing.

The lease and its effective dates

A lease, or tenancy agreement, records the right to use premises and the associated obligations. Important facts include the parties, unit, start date, charges, and permitted changes. Indexation means adjusting an amount using an agreed index, but a clause or software calculation still has to comply with applicable law.

For example

Sam signs before moving in. The agreement's signing date, start date, payment dates, and any review date describe different events. A proposed rent change must use the correct clause, rules, and effective date.

Why it matters

A date in the system can trigger invoices or notices. Copying the wrong date can affect someone's money or rights, so changes need review and a history.

Rent, service charges, and a security deposit

Rent pays for use of the premises. Service charges cover specified services or costs where the agreement and rules allow them, often through advances followed by a settlement. A security deposit is held as security and dealt with under the applicable conditions; it is not ordinary rent income.

For example

Sam's illustrative monthly charge is €900 rent plus a €100 service-cost advance. A security deposit is recorded separately. If a year's advances total €1,200 and the permitted costs properly allocated to Sam are €1,080, the €120 difference is a credit to settle.

Why it matters

Money can have different purposes even when the same organization administers it. An owner's full association contribution cannot simply be copied into the tenant's service charges.

Invoices, receipts, and reconciliation

An invoice or rent charge records an amount due. A payment receipt records money received. Reconciliation matches the two and investigates differences. It includes checking the payer, reference, period, credits, and the correct owner or organization.

For example

Mira records Sam's €1,000 monthly charge, but the bank shows €950 received. The €50 difference needs investigation. A payment might be incomplete, recorded against the wrong period, or not yet matched.

Why it matters

Creating a charge does not collect the money. Automated reminders can be wrong if the payment records are incomplete or the charge is disputed.

A repair request, a work order, and completion

A request describes a problem. A work order instructs an agreed task, with a location, responsible party, and scope. Completion means checking the agreed result and recording any remaining work. The request, repair, supplier bill, and payment are linked but separate records.

For example

Sam reports water on the ceiling. An inspection identifies a roof problem. Sending a contractor does not prove that the leak and any agreed follow-up work have been resolved.

Why it matters

Closing a ticket too early makes the dashboard look better while the person in the home still has a problem.

Planned maintenance and reserves

Planned maintenance looks ahead to work such as roof renewal, painting, or equipment replacement. A reserve is money set aside for future needs. Dutch owners' associations have reserve requirements; a multi-year maintenance plan connects anticipated work, timing, and cost.

For example

Canal Court's owners may need both a repair now and a larger roof project later. The association's plan and funds belong to its shared responsibilities, separate from Ari's rental accounts and Sam's security deposit.

Why it matters

A quiet month without repair tickets does not mean the building needs no future spending. A forecast also does not prove the required money is already available.

Reports with a clear scope and period

A report needs to say whose properties it covers, for which period, and what it measures. Occupancy describes how much space is let on a stated basis. Rent charged, cash received, costs, and property value answer other questions. Owners and managers need these distinctions to interpret results.

For example

Even if all four Canal Court apartments are let, that does not establish that all payments arrived. Ari's rental report should also be distinguishable from the owners' association's accounts.

Why it matters

Combining figures from different owners, dates, or purposes can create a convincing but misleading total. A property valuation is an estimate of value, not cash in the bank.

A little history

These selected Dutch milestones connect housing conditions, administrative tools, and tenant protections. They are not a complete history of property management. Other countries and commercial property have different histories and legal frameworks.

  1. 1901: stronger housing standards

    The Dutch Housing Act of 1901 responded to poor housing conditions and expanded the public role in housing standards and improvement.

    What this helps explain

    Property administration concerns the quality of places people use as well as the collection of payments.

    Source for 1901: stronger housing standards

  2. The 1990s: specialized property software

    As one Dutch example, Informant began developing standard property-management software in 1995 and brought it to market in 1998, according to its company history.

    What this helps explain

    Repeated tasks such as recording contracts and producing financial information became a focus for software designed around this particular trade.

    Source for The 1990s: specialized property software

  3. 2023: clearer expectations for landlord conduct

    The Dutch Good Landlordship Act took effect on 1 July 2023, addressing matters including written agreements, information for tenants, service costs, and undesirable landlord conduct.

    What this helps explain

    Clear records and communication help people understand and check how they are treated. Software must support the applicable requirements rather than assume an old template remains sufficient.

    Source for 2023: clearer expectations for landlord conduct

06

Follow one complete example

Mira manages Sam's tenancy and a roof leak

Canal Court has four separately owned apartments and shared building parts. Ari lets apartment 2 to Sam; Mira manages that rental. Assume the building documents assign the roof work in this example to the owners' association. The association has its own decision process and accounts. All amounts and arrangements below are illustrative.

  1. Identify the place, parties, and authority

    Mira links apartment 2 to the building, Ari's ownership, and her management arrangement. She records the relevant association contacts and who can authorize different kinds of work.

    Why this step matters

    Owning, living in, and managing the same place are different roles. Being able to edit a record does not itself authorize spending.

  2. Record the tenancy and hand over the home

    After the relevant checks, the parties agree the tenancy. Mira records its dates, the illustrative €900 rent and €100 service advance, and the deposit separately. The handover records the property's condition, keys, and contact arrangements.

    Why this step matters

    The recurring administration should reflect the actual agreement, with useful evidence of the starting condition.

  3. Charge correctly and match the money

    The system produces the €1,000 monthly charge. A €950 bank receipt leaves €50 to investigate. In this case Sam identifies a transfer mistake and sends the remaining €50; Mira matches both receipts to the correct charge.

    Why this step matters

    The resolution comes from checking the records and communicating, rather than treating a mismatch as automatic proof of misconduct.

  4. Investigate the reported leak

    Sam reports ceiling water. Mira assesses urgency through the agreed process, coordinates an inspection, and keeps Sam informed. The contractor identifies a shared-roof problem, so she involves the association's authorized representative.

    Why this step matters

    The report belongs with Sam's home, while the repair also belongs with a shared building part and its decision-makers.

    When things go differently

    An urgent problem needs the applicable emergency response, while responsibility and cost allocation are recorded. The system should preserve both the resident's report and the linked shared-building work.

  5. Authorize, carry out, and verify the work

    The association arranges the shared work under its procedures. Mira coordinates access and updates Sam. After repair, the agreed result and any remaining damage are checked. The appropriate reviewer checks the supplier bill against the approved work.

    Why this step matters

    An instruction, a contractor's completion message, an accepted result, and a paid bill are separate milestones.

  6. Close the records and plan ahead

    Mira records the outcome for Sam and Ari, while the association updates its own costs and maintenance plan. In the later annual service-cost review, €1,200 of Sam's advances is compared with €1,080 of permitted, properly allocated costs, producing a €120 credit to settle.

    Why this step matters

    Ari's rental finances, the association's roof spending, and Sam's service-cost statement remain distinguishable. The roof expense is not automatically passed on to Sam.

The outcome

Sam has a repaired home and understandable records. Ari can see the rental's financial position, and the association can account for shared work and future needs. Mira can trace the request, decisions, work, and bills without confusing one party's money or responsibilities with another's.

07

Value, money, and incentives

The property must work for its users

People need usable premises and dependable service. Owners need income and property that remains in good condition. Timely maintenance and clear communication can serve both interests.

For example

Resolving Sam's leak improves the home and helps prevent further building damage.

Money belongs to different activities and parties

Owners receive rent and bear relevant costs. Managers earn agreed management fees; contractors earn fees for services; software providers earn subscriptions and service fees. Associations collect contributions for shared purposes.

For example

Sam's €1,000 monthly charge is not €1,000 of revenue for Mira's management business or for its software provider.

Efficiency needs a useful definition

Less repeated data entry can reduce handling effort. But work is only improved if payments are correct, important dates are handled, and people get appropriate responses.

For example

Automatically closing repair tickets might reduce an apparent backlog without fixing any more roofs.

08

What makes this domain difficult

Maintaining a reliable history

Owners, occupants, contracts, and charges change while the building stays at the same address. Old documents may still matter.

The competing needs

A simple current view is convenient, but past periods need their own accurate context.

In practice

Replacing Ari's name after a sale must not make last year's bills appear to belong to the new owner.

How people respond, and its limits

Keep dated relationships and review changes and imported records. A shared database can spread an incorrect link as efficiently as a correct one.

Knowing who decides and who pays

A problem can affect a private unit and a shared building part. Ownership, tenancy, management authority, and insurance may point to different processes.

The competing needs

Prompt action matters, while commitments and charges must go to the correct parties.

In practice

Sam reports the leak, but the association authorizes the shared-roof work under the assumptions of this case.

How people respond, and its limits

Record responsibilities, approval limits, and escalation routes before problems arise. Handle urgency through the agreed process and investigate disputed allocation without losing the original request.

Applying local and contract-specific rules

Residential and commercial property, countries, contract dates, and property categories can have different rules.

The competing needs

Automation saves effort on repeated work, but applying one rule everywhere can produce repeated mistakes.

In practice

A rent-review date does not mean the system may apply any chosen percentage.

How people respond, and its limits

Keep reviewed rules and source dates with the relevant contract types. Have qualified reviewers check consequential changes; a technically valid calculation can still use the wrong rule.

Balancing today's bills and tomorrow's building

Large maintenance needs develop over years, while owners and associations manage nearer-term budgets.

The competing needs

Smaller contributions today may leave less money for future work; setting aside more has an immediate cost.

In practice

A patch can stop the current leak without eliminating the need for later roof renewal.

How people respond, and its limits

Connect inspections, a realistic maintenance plan, and available funds. Revisit estimates as the building changes; a plan is neither a guarantee of condition nor proof of funding.

Making financial explanations understandable

Rent, deposits, service advances, owner contributions, and supplier costs can pass through related workflows while retaining different meanings.

The competing needs

Detailed records support accuracy, but unexplained categories can overwhelm tenants and owners.

In practice

Sam needs to understand the €120 service-cost credit without reading the association's entire accounts.

How people respond, and its limits

Use clear statements with traceable calculations and role-appropriate access. Keep human help available for questions; a portal or AI-written explanation cannot correct a misclassified cost by itself.

09

Common misunderstandings

The manager owns the property and can decide everything.

A better way to understand it

Management authority comes from an arrangement and the applicable rules. The owner, association, or another party may need to decide.

Every amount received is rent income.

A better way to understand it

Deposits, advances, and money administered for others have different purposes. Record their meaning and the party they belong to.

A fully let building means all the money has arrived.

A better way to understand it

Occupancy and payment collection measure different things. Both need a clear reporting period and scope.

A contractor's bill proves the repair is complete.

A better way to understand it

The bill must be checked against the work and agreed result. A resident's unresolved problem should remain visible.

10

The ideas to take with you

  • Connect each place to the right people, agreement, period, and responsibility.

  • Keep rent, service costs, deposits, and association funds understandable and distinguishable.

  • Follow a repair from report to verified result, alongside its approvals and bills.

  • Good software supports dependable service and traceable decisions over the life of the property.

11

Try it for yourself

Pause and think through each situation, then open the answer.

1. Sam reports ceiling water in apartment 2, but inspection identifies a shared-roof problem. Should the record simply be moved away from Sam's home?

Reveal answer to question 1

Keep Sam's report and link it to the shared-building work and the correct decision-makers.

The reasoning

The affected location, cause, and responsibility are related facts. Losing the original report would make follow-up with Sam harder.

2. The system records a €1,000 charge and a €950 receipt. What should Mira do before treating the €50 difference as an overdue payment?

Reveal answer to question 2

Check the period, reference, other receipts, credits, due date, and any dispute, and communicate as needed.

The reasoning

The difference needs reconciliation. A partial match alone does not establish why it exists.

3. Sam paid €1,200 in service advances and the permitted costs properly allocated to Sam are €1,080. What does the difference represent?

Reveal answer to question 3

A €120 credit to be settled through the applicable process, not additional rent income.

The reasoning

The advance and the supported final cost are different figures. The statement should make their relationship clear.

4. The roof contractor sends a completion message, but agreed follow-up work remains. Is the entire case complete?

Reveal answer to question 4

No. Record what was completed and keep the remaining work and communication visible.

The reasoning

A contractor update, an accepted result, and financial settlement are separate milestones.

12

Questions to ask a practitioner

  • How do you distinguish owners, occupants, managers, and associations in your records?

  • Which decisions can staff make directly, and which need approval?

  • Can a tenant or owner trace a charge to its agreement and supporting calculation?

  • How do maintenance plans relate to available funds and current building condition?

  • What stays linked when ownership, tenancy, or software systems change?
