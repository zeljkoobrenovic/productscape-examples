Use case: scientific-educational
Asset type: one illustration in a plain-language product-domain tutorial for adults.
Domain: Selling under your own name: how a merchant commerce platform works
Reader: A curious adult coming from a different field who has never run a shop online. No knowledge of retail, payments, or software is assumed.
Scope: An introduction to platforms that let independent merchants run their own branded shop online and in person, using Shopify as the best-known example. It follows one order from a shopper's first visit to the merchant being paid, and covers the platform's role in checkout, payments, stock, shipping, and its ecosystem of add-on apps. Marketplaces where the platform sells to the shopper itself (such as Amazon's retail business), warehouse operations, and detailed tax or customs law are outside this guide. Payment rules, taxes, and import duties differ by country; examples that depend on them are marked as local. The shop, people, and numbers in the walkthrough are fictional.
Subject: The people involved and how they relate
The merchant: Owns the brand and products, sets prices, and is legally the seller. Ranges from a single founder working from home to a large retailer with hundreds of stores. Cares about: Turning visitors into paying customers, keeping fees and fraud losses low, getting paid quickly, and spending less time on admin. Larger merchants also care about connecting to their existing stock and accounting systems.
The shopper: Buys from the merchant, often on a phone, and often having arrived from a social media post, a search result, or an AI assistant's suggestion. Cares about: A quick and trustworthy checkout, an accurate delivery promise, easy returns, and not having to type card and address details again.
The platform: Runs the shop software, checkout, and order records for millions of merchants, and usually also processes payments. It is not the seller of the goods. Cares about: Merchants growing and staying, because its income rises with their sales. It must also keep fraud and failed payments low across the whole network.
Payment and delivery partners: Card networks, banks, and payment processors move the money; carriers move the parcels. The platform connects to them on the merchant's behalf. Cares about: Their own rules and fees. A card network can fine or cut off merchants with too many disputed payments; a carrier charges by weight, distance, and speed.
App developers and agencies: Independent companies that build add-ons (for reviews, subscriptions, loyalty points, and much more) or design and run shops for merchants. Cares about: Reaching merchants through the platform's app store and being paid reliably. They depend on the platform not changing its rules or copying their product.

Teaching scene:
A relationship map. Center: 'Merchant' (ceramics studio). Left: 'Shopper' with arrows 'buys from' to merchant and 'pays' to 'Payment partners' (bank, card network). Top: 'Platform' with arrows to merchant labeled 'shop software, checkout, payouts' and from merchant labeled 'subscription + fees'. Right: 'App developers' with arrow to merchant 'add-ons' and to platform 'revenue share'. Bottom: 'Carrier' with arrow from merchant 'parcel' to shopper. Cash arrows in green, service arrows in blue, all labeled.

Style and composition:
- Aspect ratio 16:9, generous margins, bright warm-white background.
- A polished adult educational illustration: flat editorial drawing, crisp navy outlines,
  restrained teal, blue, and warm amber accents, subtle texture, very little shading.
- Make the concept understandable at the width of a reading column and on a phone.
- Use a small number of recognizable objects and people. Keep relationships spatially clear.
- Use an inclusive cast without occupational or cultural stereotypes.
- Short everyday labels are welcome where they teach the distinction. Use only labels
  explicitly requested in the teaching scene; otherwise keep the image free of text.
- Arrows must have a clear meaning and direction; do not connect unrelated comparison
  panels as though they are required stages. Do not imply certainty, causality, proportions,
  cash movements, or guaranteed outcomes that the explanation does not support.
- Keep all important content inside the frame. No headline, watermark, brand logo,
  decorative charts, invented statistics, jargon, tiny writing, photorealism, or 3D rendering.
- Explain the idea visually. The surrounding tutorial provides its full definition and caveats.
