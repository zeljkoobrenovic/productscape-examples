A beginner's guide

From license plate to sold car: how vehicle data powers the used-car trade

About 22 min to readUpdated 2026-10-10

Who this is for

A curious adult from a different field who has bought or sold a car at most a few times. No knowledge of the car trade, Dutch regulation, or software is assumed.

What this guide covers

An introduction to how professional used-car traders in the Netherlands use vehicle data and official registration services to buy, price, sell and transfer cars, and how private buyers use the same data to check a car. The Dutch rules (the RDW register, odometer registration, BPM tax) are local; other countries organise similar work differently. New-car sales, repairs, insurance claims handling and car financing itself are left out. VWE Automotive is one Dutch provider in this field; the explanation is meant to hold for the trade in general. The people and the car in the walkthrough are fictional, and its amounts are illustrative.

01

What you will learn

  • Explain why a license plate is a key to information, and who keeps that information.

  • Distinguish what a car is (its specification), what happened to it (its history), and what it is worth (its value).

  • Follow a used car from trade-in to a new owner and explain where data changes a decision.

  • Explain why owning a car and being registered as its keeper are separate, and why transferring the registration matters to the seller.

  • Describe the tension between open public data, paid data services and the privacy of car owners.

02

Start with the everyday need

A used car has no instruction leaflet about its past. A buyer sees paint, seats and a dashboard, but not whether the car was in a serious accident in another country, whether its odometer was turned back, or whether a similar car sells for much less two towns away. The vehicle-data domain answers those questions by connecting the license plate to official records, partner databases and market information, and turning the answers into decisions: what to pay, what to ask, and how to complete the legal paperwork.

Most of the work is done by and for professionals: car dealers, traders who buy and sell across borders, lease companies that sell cars when contracts end, and software makers who build these answers into their own tools. Private buyers use a lighter version, often through a phone app.

In the Netherlands, many more cars change hands second-hand than are sold new, and each sale carries money at risk on both sides. A dealer who pays too much for a trade-in loses margin; a buyer who misses hidden damage pays for it later. The same data also feeds official steps: a car cannot legally change owner without a registration transfer, and an imported car cannot be registered without paying the Dutch registration tax. Getting these steps right protects people from fines, fraud and unpleasant surprises.

A familiar comparison

Think of buying a house. You look at it, but you also check the land registry, a surveyor's report and recent sale prices of similar houses nearby, and a notary records the change of ownership.

Where the comparison stops

A car moves, crosses borders and wears out in a few years rather than decades. Its history may be spread across several countries' records. Thousands of cars are valued every day, so checks must take seconds, not weeks. And there is no notary: the dealer usually performs the official registration transfer at the counter, online, in minutes.

03

The people involved

The dealer

Buys cars (often as trade-ins), prepares and advertises them, sells them to private buyers, and handles the paperwork. Ranges from a one-site garage with 40 cars to a group with dozens of branches.

What matters to them

Buying at a price that leaves room for repairs and profit, selling before the car stands too long, and completing registration correctly so nothing comes back to haunt them.

The private buyer and seller

Sells their old car to a dealer and buys a 'new' used one, or trades privately with another person.

What matters to them

A fair price, an honest car, and understandable information. They rarely see the professional tools behind the dealer's offer.

The trader and the lease company

Traders move cars between dealers and countries; lease companies own large fleets and must sell thousands of cars when contracts end.

What matters to them

Correct tax on imports and exports, fast paperwork, and the best price for cars sold in bulk.

The vehicle authority (RDW)

The Dutch government agency that keeps the national vehicle register, issues registration documents, records odometer readings and recognises companies allowed to perform official transactions.

What matters to them

A reliable, fraud-resistant register and lawful use of the personal data in it.

The data provider

Companies such as VWE, Autotelex or international groups that combine official records with partner and market data and sell it as reports, software, transaction services and APIs (machine connections between software systems).

What matters to them

Accurate, available data; licence terms they may pass on; and products customers will pay for even though some basic data is free.

04

The key concepts

License plate and the vehicle register

The plate (kenteken) is a short code linked to a record in the national register: the car's technical facts, registration dates, inspection results and who is registered as its keeper.

For example

Typing a plate into a lookup service returns the make, model, fuel type, first registration date and when the annual roadworthiness test (APK) expires.

Why it matters

The plate is how almost every check starts. Because the register is official, it is the anchor that other data sources are matched against.

VIN

The Vehicle Identification Number: a 17-character code stamped into the car by the manufacturer. Unlike a plate, it never changes when the car moves to another country.

For example

A Golf imported from Germany gets a new Dutch plate, but its VIN still connects it to its German service and damage records.

Why it matters

Foreign history and exact factory options are usually found by VIN, not by plate.

Specification, history and value

Three different questions. Specification is what the car is (exact edition, engine, factory options). History is what happened to it (damage, maintenance, odometer readings, previous owners). Value is what it is worth today, given both and the current market.

For example

Two cars with the same model name can differ by thousands of euros in value because one has a larger engine and a navigation package, and the other was repaired after an accident.

Why it matters

Mixing them up leads to bad decisions: a perfect history does not make a basic edition worth as much as a fully equipped one.

Odometer judgement

Garages report the odometer reading when they service or test a car. Those readings are stored centrally, and each new reading is compared with earlier ones. A sequence that goes up plausibly is 'logical'; one that drops or jumps oddly is 'illogical'.

For example

Readings of 60,000, 85,000 and then 70,000 km mark the car as illogical, a warning that the counter may have been turned back.

Why it matters

Mileage strongly drives price, and turning back a counter is cheap to do. The judgement protects buyers, but it only covers readings that were actually recorded.

Valuation and maximum purchase price

A valuation estimates what a car would sell for, based on comparable cars currently offered and recently sold. The maximum purchase price works backwards from that: expected sale price minus repairs, costs and the margin the dealer needs.

For example

If similar cars sell for 18,000 euros, reconditioning costs 900 and the dealer wants 1,800 margin, the dealer should not pay much more than about 15,300.

Why it matters

Dealers make or lose their money when they buy, not when they sell. A value without a buying limit invites overpaying.

Ownership versus registration (tenaamstelling and vrijwaring)

Owning a car and being its registered keeper are separate. When a car is sold, the registration must be transferred to the new keeper (tenaamstelling). When a dealer buys a car, the seller receives proof that the registration was transferred to the trade (vrijwaring, an indemnification), so later fines and taxes no longer reach them.

For example

A seller who hands over the keys but whose name remains in the register can still receive the new driver's speeding fines and road tax bills.

Why it matters

The legal handover happens in the register, not when money and keys change hands. Recognised companies can perform these transactions themselves.

BPM

A Dutch tax paid once when a passenger car is first registered in the Netherlands. For an imported used car, the tax is reduced for its age and depreciation, using a fixed table, a market price list or an individual valuation report.

For example

A trader importing a three-year-old car from Germany must pay the remaining BPM before it gets a Dutch plate; choosing a well-founded valuation can lower the amount legitimately.

Why it matters

BPM can be thousands of euros, so a mistake can wipe out the profit on an imported car. Exporters can sometimes reclaim part of it.

05

A little history

These Dutch turning points explain why today's trade relies on the register and specialised data. They are a selection, not a complete history of vehicle data, and the lessons are interpretations.

  1. 1993: a registration tax tied to the register

    BPM replaced the earlier special consumption taxes on cars and motorcycles. It became payable when a car is entered into the Dutch register.

    What this helps explain

    Because tax depends on registration, importing a used car became a calculation as much as a purchase, which created demand for reliable BPM and valuation services.

    Source for 1993: a registration tax tied to the register

  2. 2006 to 2014: odometer readings move from voluntary to mandatory

    From 2006 the Nationale AutoPas foundation collected odometer readings voluntarily reported by garages. From 1 January 2014 recording them became a legal obligation at the RDW, which took over the earlier records. Parliament's evaluation estimated that the share of 'illogical' readings fell from over 5% in spring 2011 to about 2% in spring 2015.

    What this helps explain

    A shared, mandatory record turned a common fraud into something buyers can check in seconds. It also shows how regulation, not just technology, changed the trade.

    Source for 2006 to 2014: odometer readings move from voluntary to mandatory

  3. 2012 to 2014: basic register data becomes open

    The RDW began piloting open vehicle data in 2012 and now publishes register datasets free of charge and without restriction.

    What this helps explain

    Basic facts about a car became free for anyone to build an app on. Paid providers had to earn their money through history, valuation, workflow and official transactions instead.

    Source for 2012 to 2014: basic register data becomes open

  4. 2014: the registration card and transfer code

    The two-part paper registration certificate was replaced by a credit-card-sized registration card plus a separate code needed to transfer the car. Cards are produced centrally by the RDW rather than at dealers or post offices.

    What this helps explain

    Transfers became harder to fake and easier to do digitally, which moved more paperwork into dealers' own software.

    Source for 2014: the registration card and transfer code

  5. 2020s: consolidation of portals and data providers

    Advertising portals and data companies are merging. For example, trade press reported that AutoScout24 took over the Dutch portals AutoTrack and Gaspedaal.nl in 2025, and VWE acquired the BPM and valuation specialist XRAY Automotive in 2026.

    What this helps explain

    Fewer, larger players increasingly offer the data, the advertising and the dealer software together. Dealers gain convenience but depend more on a few companies.

    Source for 2020s: consolidation of portals and data providers

06

Follow one complete example

Sanne takes a Golf in trade and sells it to Priya

This is a fictional teaching case with illustrative amounts. Sanne runs an independent used-car business with about 60 cars in stock. Mark wants to trade in his 2019 Volkswagen Golf for a newer car from her lot. The Golf was originally imported from Germany. Sanne uses a data provider's web platform and phone app for checks, pricing, adverts and registration.

  1. Identify the car

    Sanne scans the Golf's plate with her phone. The register returns the model, fuel type, first registration and test date. A VIN lookup adds the exact edition and factory options: this one has the larger engine and a navigation package.

    Why this step matters

    Starting with the right specification prevents valuing the wrong car. The options Mark mentioned are now confirmed rather than taken on trust.

  2. Check what happened to it

    She orders a history report. The odometer judgement is logical. The damage history, which searches by VIN in foreign databases, shows a repaired rear-end collision in Germany four years ago.

    Why this step matters

    The damage happened before the car came to the Netherlands, so Dutch records alone would not have shown it.

    When things go differently

    A history report only shows what was recorded. Sanne still inspects the car, and her app calculates a repair estimate from photos of a paint mismatch on the rear bumper.

  3. Value it and make an offer

    The valuation compares the Golf with similar cars currently advertised and recently sold, and corrects for the damage history. Similar cars sell for about 18,000 euros. With 900 euros of reconditioning and her target margin, the app suggests a maximum purchase price of about 15,300. Sanne offers 15,000 and prints the appraisal for Mark.

    Why this step matters

    Mark expected 16,500 from a consumer website. The printed comparison and the documented German damage explain the difference, so the negotiation is about facts instead of opinion.

    When things go differently

    If Sanne had skipped the history report, she might have paid the higher price and discovered the damage only when a buyer's own check found it.

  4. Take the car into stock legally

    Mark accepts. Sanne performs the indemnification transaction directly with the RDW from her software, and Mark receives proof that the car is no longer registered to him. The Golf gets a digital file that collects every report and document.

    Why this step matters

    Mark is now protected from fines and taxes caused by later drivers, and Sanne holds a record of what she knew when she bought.

  5. Price and advertise

    Sanne sets an asking price of 18,250, slightly below comparable cars because of the damage history, and publishes the advert once. The platform sends it to many advertising sites and her own website. After three weeks, a nightly check flags that it has fewer viewings than similar cars, and she lowers the price by 300 euros.

    Why this step matters

    Each extra week in stock ties up money and the car loses value. Market data turns 'gut feel' pricing into a deliberate choice.

  6. Sell and transfer

    Priya finds the advert. Before visiting, she runs a paid consumer report on the plate and sees the same German damage, which the advert already disclosed. She buys the Golf. Sanne prepares the sales contract and invoice, and transfers the registration to Priya's name in a few minutes. Priya drives away the same day.

    Why this step matters

    Because seller and buyer looked at the same facts, there is no surprise and little room for a dispute later.

    When things go differently

    If the registration system is unavailable on a busy Saturday, the transfer must wait. Good dealer software queues the transaction and Sanne explains the delay instead of handing over a car that is still registered to her company.

The outcome

Mark received a fair, explained price and legal protection. Sanne bought below her limit, sold within about a month and kept a record of every step. Priya bought a car whose past she understood. Success here means that the money, the legal registration and everyone's knowledge of the car stayed in step.

07

Value, money, and incentives

Data is worth what it changes

A report earns its price when it changes a decision: a lower offer, a car not bought, a dispute avoided. That is why dealers pay per report or per month for history and valuations, while basic plate facts, which rarely change a decision, are free.

For example

A 10-euro damage report that prevents overpaying by 1,500 euros is easy to justify. Some providers charge nothing when no damage is found, so the dealer pays only when the report is informative.

Who pays, and how

Dealers and lease companies pay monthly subscriptions for software modules plus fees per report or per official transaction. Software makers and insurers pay for API access and licences. Consumers pay small amounts per report. Providers in turn pay the government and partner sources for some data, and spend heavily on keeping systems available and accurate.

For example

A dealer's monthly bill may combine a stock-management subscription, twenty registration transfers and a handful of history reports.

Interests that pull apart

Sellers want high prices; buyers and dealers want complete information. Advertising portals want dealers to use their own tools; independent providers want to stay neutral across portals. Data providers want to use owner information for marketing, while car owners and regulators want that information protected.

For example

Predicting which owners are likely to change cars soon is valuable to dealers, but it uses data about people, so it is only acceptable under strict purpose rules.

08

What makes this domain difficult

History is spread across borders and sources

A car's damage and service records may sit with insurers, repairers and manufacturers in several countries, each with different access terms and formats. Imported cars are where the gaps are largest.

The competing needs

More sources mean better coverage but higher costs, more licence conditions and slower reports.

In practice

Sanne found the German collision only because her provider searched foreign databases by VIN.

How people respond, and its limits

Providers combine many partner sources and say which countries a report covers. A report with no findings still does not prove a car has no history, so physical inspection remains necessary.

Free data sets a low price floor

The government publishes basic register data for free, so anyone can build a plate-check app. Customers ask why they should pay.

The competing needs

Charging for basic facts drives users to free apps; giving everything away leaves nothing to fund history sources, valuation models and support.

In practice

Priya could see the Golf's make and test date for free; she paid only for the history and odometer judgement.

How people respond, and its limits

Providers charge for what free data cannot do: combined history, valuations, official transactions and workflow inside dealer software. Each new free source pushes them to move further up.

Everything depends on one official register

Registration transfers, odometer judgements and much of the data come from the government authority's systems. If its connection is down, many companies stop at once.

The competing needs

Copying register data locally keeps lookups working but risks stale information; official transactions cannot be done from a copy at all.

In practice

A Saturday outage means dealers cannot hand over sold cars legally until it is restored.

How people respond, and its limits

Providers keep a recent copy for lookups, queue transactions to send when the connection returns, and inform customers quickly. Transfers still wait for the authority.

Privacy versus usefulness

The register links cars to people. Knowing who owns which car, and when they might sell, is commercially valuable and also sensitive personal data.

The competing needs

Stricter limits protect owners but reduce what dealers and importers can do with the data; looser limits risk fines and loss of public trust.

In practice

A consumer report shows a car's past, but not the names of previous owners.

How people respond, and its limits

Data is supplied only for documented purposes, owner details are removed or pseudonymised (replaced with codes), and every query is logged. Rules differ between countries and change over time.

Valuations must keep up with a moving market

Prices shift with fuel prices, tax changes, supply and new technology. Used electric cars, for example, raise a new question: how healthy is the battery?

The competing needs

Reacting quickly to market changes makes values more current but also noisier; smoothing them makes them stable but slower to warn dealers.

In practice

Two identical used electric cars can deserve different prices if one battery has lost much more capacity.

How people respond, and its limits

Models are retrained frequently and checked against actual sale prices, and new measurements such as battery health tests are added. A valuation remains an estimate, which is why dealers still apply judgement.

09

Common misunderstandings

If the plate check is clean, the car is fine.

A better way to understand it

Checks show only what was recorded. Damage repaired privately, or history in a country the report does not cover, will not appear. A check reduces risk; it does not replace inspection.

A car is sold when the money and keys change hands.

A better way to understand it

Legally, the handover is complete when the registration is transferred. Until then, the previous keeper can receive fines and tax bills.

The value of a car is a fixed number you can look up.

A better way to understand it

A valuation is an estimate that depends on exact specification, history, mileage, region and timing. Dealers also need a buying limit, which is lower than the expected sale price.

Vehicle data companies own the register.

A better way to understand it

The government authority keeps the register. Providers are recognised to access and use it under conditions, and they add value by combining it with other sources and tools.

10

The ideas to take with you

  • The license plate (and for foreign history, the VIN) is the key that connects a physical car to official records, partner databases and market information.

  • Specification, history and value answer different questions; good decisions need all three.

  • Dealers make their money when they buy, so a maximum purchase price matters as much as a valuation.

  • Ownership changes legally in the register, and recognised companies perform those transfers within their own software.

  • Free public data covers the basics, so paid services must earn their place through history, valuation, official transactions and workflow, while respecting owners' privacy.

11

Try it for yourself

Pause and think through each situation, then open the answer.

1. Why did Sanne's check find the Golf's damage when a Dutch-only check might not have?

Reveal answer to question 1

Because the damage happened in Germany before import, and her provider searched foreign records by the car's VIN.

The reasoning

The plate changed when the car was imported, but the VIN did not. Foreign history is linked to the VIN, so a plate-only, Dutch-only search can miss it.

2. Mark hands over his keys and receives the money, but nobody transfers the registration. What risk does Mark still carry?

Reveal answer to question 2

He can still receive fines and tax bills caused by later use of the car.

The reasoning

The register, not the handshake, decides who is responsible. The indemnification proof protects the seller once the registration has moved to the trade.

3. Similar cars sell for 20,000 euros. Repairs will cost 1,000 and the dealer needs 2,000 margin. Why should the dealer not offer 20,000 for the trade-in?

Reveal answer to question 3

Because the maximum purchase price is about 17,000 euros; paying more leaves no money for repairs or profit.

The reasoning

The value is what the car may sell for. The buying limit works backwards from it by subtracting costs and margin.

4. Basic plate data is free from the government. Why do dealers still pay a data provider?

Reveal answer to question 4

Because the provider supplies what changes decisions: history from many sources, valuations, official transactions and tools that fit their daily work.

The reasoning

Free data answers what the car is. Dealers pay for answers about what happened to it, what it is worth, and getting the legal steps done quickly.

12

Questions to ask a practitioner

  • Which countries does your damage history cover, and how do you tell a customer when a source has no data?

  • How do you check whether a valuation was right after the car is sold?

  • What happens to your customers when the government register connection is down?

  • Which owner-related data do you refuse to use, and who decides?

  • How will used electric cars change the way you value and check cars?
