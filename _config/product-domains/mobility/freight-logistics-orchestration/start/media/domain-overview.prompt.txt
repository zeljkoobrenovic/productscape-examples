Draw the picture described below for the top of a product-domain website.
Domain: Freight Logistics Orchestration
Reader: a product manager or engineer new to this domain who needs the big picture in ten seconds.

Background (for understanding only; do not write it in the image):
What the domain is: Covers the shipper, carrier, broker, managed transportation, TMS, contract logistics, visibility, rating, tendering, settlement, and operations workflows required to plan, procure, execute, and optimize freight across truckload, LTL, intermodal, cross-border, and warehousing networks.
Customers: Shipper Demand: Enterprise Transportation Leader; Carrier Supply: Fleet Owner and Carrier Dispatcher; Freight Operations: Logistics Operations Specialist
Products: Shipper Transportation Control Tower (B2B); Carrier Network Experience (B2B2B); Freight Operations Workbench (B2B); Freight Platform Foundations (platform)
Product bricks: 32 building blocks in groups: Shipper Control Tower, Capacity Marketplace, Execution and Visibility, Financial and Intelligence Foundations
Data: People, Accounts, and Access Data, Commercial and Financial Data, Operational Workflow and Event Data, Analytics, Intelligence, and Sustainability Data
Delivery channels: Cloud Runtime Environments: Core Freight Runtime; Web Workspaces: Shipper Workspace, Carrier Experience, Freight Operations Workbench; API and EDI Integration: EDI and API Gateway
External systems: ERP systems: ERP and order management systems, ERP, WMS, OMS, and EDI gateways, Customer ERP, WMS, OMS, and finance systems, Customer ERP and finance systems; Data providers: Freight market data providers, Government authority and insurance data sources, Fuel index data providers, Emissions factor datasets; Telematics: ELD and telematics partners, Trailer telematics and yard systems, Telematics, ELD, mobile, rail, ocean, and parcel visibility feeds, Mobile, ELD, and telematics providers; Shipping and logistics: Carrier EDI and API endpoints; Shipping and logistics: Warehouse dock scheduling systems; AI models: OCR and document AI providers; Messaging services: Email, SMS, voice, EDI, and customer messaging systems; Payment providers: Payment processors, banks, ERP, and tax systems; Identity providers: Identity, sanctions, insurance, and carrier authority data providers; Analytics: Operational databases and data warehouse
Competitors: 8 players, for example Uber Freight, C.H. Robinson, Ryder System

Composition:
One wide "context map" read from left to right:
- Left, outside the boundary: each customer group as ONE small cluster of two or three generic people
  with exactly one label, the group name. Do not split a group into individual roles or label single people.
- Center: one large rounded boundary for the domain. Inside it, the products as a row of distinct
  product cards on top; below them ONE shared row of building-block piles, one pile per product brick
  group, spanning the full width under all products. Products are assembled from many groups and groups
  are shared by many products, so do not pair a product with a group: no columns and no product-to-group
  arrows; the product row simply rests on the brick row. Below the bricks a shared data layer (a simple
  database shape) used by all brick groups.
- Along the bottom edge of the boundary: the delivery channels as a base strip of runtime platforms
  (servers, cloud, app stores) that the bricks are deployed to and the products are delivered through.
- Right, outside the boundary: the external systems as separate service tiles, each connected to the
  boundary with a two-way connector line.
- Bottom right, set apart and smaller: competitors as alternative storefronts the customers could choose instead,
  with a thin dashed line towards the customers, not connected to the domain.
- Arrows: customers <-> products; the brick row reads and writes the data layer; bricks are deployed onto the delivery channels; the domain exchanges data with the external systems. No other arrows.
