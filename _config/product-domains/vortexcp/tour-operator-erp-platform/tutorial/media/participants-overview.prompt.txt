Use case: scientific-educational
Asset type: one illustration in a plain-language product-domain tutorial for adults.
Domain: From a travel idea to a trip that works
Reader: Someone arriving from another field, with no assumed knowledge of travel businesses, accounting, or business software.
Scope: An introduction to the work of tour operators and the software that connects planning, sales, suppliers, travellers, and finance. Examples reflect group and tailor-made travel in a European setting. Legal duties depend on the arrangement, country, and applicable dates. This is not a guide to running an airline or choosing a holiday. Nora, Coast & Village Travel, the trip, and all prices and contract terms are fictional.
Subject: The people involved and how they relate
Travellers: Choose and pay for a trip, provide needed details, and experience the result. The person booking may be arranging travel for several people. Cares about: An accurate description, a workable trip, clear terms, and timely help when something changes.
The tour operator: Designs trips, agrees supplier terms, sells places, coordinates delivery, and manages the finances. Nora is a trip planner at fictional Coast & Village Travel. Cares about: Keeping promises while covering costs. Sales, operations, service, and finance colleagues need compatible versions of the same information.
Suppliers and destination partners: Provide accommodation, transport, guiding, or activities. A destination management company is a local business that can coordinate several such services for the operator. Cares about: Clear requests, realistic timings, confirmed quantities, and payment under the agreed terms.
Travel agents and other resellers: Help customers choose and book travel supplied by another business. Their exact commercial and legal role depends on the arrangement. Cares about: Reliable availability, clear booking status, and any commission: a fee earned for selling the trip.
The software provider: Supplies and supports the business system, websites, or portals. The travel organization pays for access and often for setup or additional services. Cares about: Reliable workflows, usable connections to other systems, and customers who can operate the software confidently. It does not own the hotel rooms or deliver the holiday.

Teaching scene:
An adult teaching map about coordination, not money or ownership. Center a woman at a desk labeled 'Tour operator'. Around her four separate cards: travellers with luggage labeled 'Travellers'; hotel, minibus and guide labeled 'Suppliers and local partners'; person at travel desk labeled 'Travel agents'; laptop support person labeled 'Software provider'. Exactly four connections, each ONLY between the center and one card. Traveller connection label 'Plans and updates'; supplier connection label 'Requests and confirmations'; agent connection label 'Booking details'; software connection label 'Tools and support'. Use plain lines without arrowheads: these describe coordination relationships, not a complete delivery route. No links between outside cards. No money, prices, ownership symbols or decorative extra words. Large legible labels, warm white, navy, teal and restrained amber.

Style and composition:
- Aspect ratio 16:9, generous margins, bright warm-white background.
- A polished adult educational illustration: flat editorial drawing, crisp navy outlines,
  restrained teal, blue, and warm amber accents, subtle texture, very little shading.
- Make the concept understandable at the width of a reading column and on a phone.
- Use a small number of recognizable objects and people. Keep relationships spatially clear.
- Use an inclusive cast without occupational or cultural stereotypes.
- Short everyday labels are welcome where they teach the distinction. Use only labels
  explicitly requested in the teaching scene; otherwise keep the image free of text.
- Arrows must have a clear meaning and direction; do not connect unrelated comparison
  panels as though they are required stages. Do not imply certainty, causality, proportions,
  cash movements, or guaranteed outcomes that the explanation does not support.
- Keep all important content inside the frame. No headline, watermark, brand logo,
  decorative charts, invented statistics, jargon, tiny writing, photorealism, or 3D rendering.
- Explain the idea visually. The surrounding tutorial provides its full definition and caveats.
