Use case: scientific-educational
Asset type: one illustration in a plain-language product-domain tutorial for adults.
Domain: Selling under your own name: how a merchant commerce platform works
Reader: A curious adult coming from a different field who has never run a shop online. No knowledge of retail, payments, or software is assumed.
Scope: An introduction to platforms that let independent merchants run their own branded shop online and in person, using Shopify as the best-known example. It follows one order from a shopper's first visit to the merchant being paid, and covers the platform's role in checkout, payments, stock, shipping, and its ecosystem of add-on apps. Marketplaces where the platform sells to the shopper itself (such as Amazon's retail business), warehouse operations, and detailed tax or customs law are outside this guide. Payment rules, taxes, and import duties differ by country; examples that depend on them are marked as local. The shop, people, and numbers in the walkthrough are fictional.
Subject: Two mugs, a market stall, and the last blue bowl (fictional)
Setup: Lena runs a one-person ceramics brand from her studio. She uses a commerce platform for her website, takes card payments at a weekend market with the platform's card reader, and has installed a reviews app. Sam, a shopper in another city, sees a video of her blue bowls on social media. Lena, Sam, and all amounts are invented for illustration.
Step 1: Sam arrives at the storefront. Sam taps the link in the video and lands on Lena's product page. The page shows photos, the price, reviews from earlier buyers, and that one blue bowl is left. The platform served the page quickly on a phone and the reviews app supplied the ratings. Why: Lena paid for the visit with her own marketing. The platform's job is not to wreck that effort with a slow page or a confusing layout. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 2: Sam goes to checkout. Sam adds the blue bowl and two mugs. At checkout the platform recognizes Sam's phone number from a saved wallet used at another shop on the same platform, fills in the address and card, and shows shipping and sales tax for that address. Total: 92 dollars. Why: Sam never typed a card number, which raises the chance the sale completes. Tax and shipping are calculated by the platform, but Lena, as merchant of record, is still responsible for them being right. Possible complication: A surprise shipping cost at this step is one of the most common reasons shoppers give up.
Step 3: The payment is authorized and checked. Sam's bank authorizes 92 dollars. In parallel, the platform's fraud checks compare the order with patterns seen across many merchants: is this card, device, and address combination normal? The order is scored low-risk and marked paid. Why: Authorization reserves Sam's money; it has not reached Lena. The fraud check protects Lena from a chargeback weeks later, which a single small merchant could not judge well from her own few orders. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 4: The exception: the last bowl sells twice. At the same moment, Lena's friend is minding the market stall, where the one remaining blue bowl is on display. A visitor buys it with the card reader. Because the stall and the website share one stock count, whichever sale is recorded first claims the bowl. The website sale was recorded a few seconds earlier, so the reader warns that the bowl is sold, and Lena's friend apologizes and offers a different bowl. Why: Without a shared count, both sales would succeed and one customer would get a cancellation days later. Shared stock across channels is valuable precisely because these collisions happen. Possible complication: Shared counting only works if every channel records sales promptly. A till working offline with a bad connection can still oversell, and someone must decide which customer to disappoint.
Step 5: Lena packs and ships. Lena sees the order in her admin screen, buys a discounted shipping label through the platform, and prints it. Marking the order shipped captures the payment and sends Sam a tracking link. Why: Capture happens at shipping so Sam is not charged for something that never leaves. The carrier now holds the delivery promise, but Sam will still blame Lena if it is late. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 6: Lena is paid. Two days later the platform pays out Lena's week of sales in one transfer. From Sam's 92 dollars it has subtracted a payment processing fee, and the shipping label cost was billed separately. Lena's monthly subscription and her reviews app appear on a separate platform bill. Why: Lena's real margin depends on all these deductions together. Payout timing also matters: a small business may need this week's sales to buy next week's clay. Possible complication: None specified.
Outcome: Sam receives the bowl and mugs, leaves a review that will help the next shopper decide, and is now in Lena's customer list for future emails. Lena earned a sale she could not have handled alone, and avoided a double sale. The platform earned fees from the subscription, the payment, and the shipping label, and its fraud checks learned from one more normal order. Success for everyone means the same thing: the promise made on the product page was kept.

Teaching scene:
A six-panel comic strip in reading order, same two characters throughout (Lena the ceramicist with hair in a bun and an apron; Sam the shopper with curly hair). 1 Sam on a phone sees a video of a blue bowl, label 'One blue bowl left'. 2 Sam's phone checkout with saved wallet, label 'Total $92'. 3 Shopper's bank icon 'Authorized' and a shield 'Fraud check: low risk'. 4 Complication: at a market stall, Lena's friend shows a card reader reading 'Sold online' to a visitor and offers a different bowl. 5 Lena packs the bowl and two mugs and prints a shipping label, label 'Shipped = payment captured'. 6 Two days later Lena checks her phone, label 'Payout: this week's sales minus fees'. No other numbers or amounts anywhere except $92.

Style and composition:
- Aspect ratio 16:9, generous margins, bright warm-white background.
- A polished adult educational illustration: flat editorial drawing, crisp navy outlines,
  restrained teal, blue, and warm amber accents, subtle texture, very little shading.
- Make the concept understandable at the width of a reading column and on a phone.
- Use a small number of recognizable objects and people. Keep relationships spatially clear.
- Use an inclusive cast without occupational or cultural stereotypes.
- Short everyday labels are welcome where they teach the distinction. Use only labels
  explicitly requested in the teaching scene; otherwise keep the image free of text.
- Arrows must have a clear meaning and direction; do not connect unrelated comparison
  panels as though they are required stages. Do not imply certainty, causality, proportions,
  cash movements, or guaranteed outcomes that the explanation does not support.
- Keep all important content inside the frame. No headline, watermark, brand logo,
  decorative charts, invented statistics, jargon, tiny writing, photorealism, or 3D rendering.
- Explain the idea visually. The surrounding tutorial provides its full definition and caveats.
