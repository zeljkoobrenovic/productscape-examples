A beginner's guide

How a group of commerce software companies serves the same merchants: an introduction to the Katapull Commerce Hub

About 26 min to readUpdated 2026-11-04

Who this is for

A curious adult from another field, for example finance, consulting or general management, who has bought things online but has never worked inside a webshop, warehouse or wholesale business. No software or logistics knowledge is assumed.

What this guide covers

This tutorial explains the chain of software a mid-sized Dutch or Belgian merchant uses to sell goods and get them to buyers: shop, marketplaces, store till, business administration, trade documents, warehouse, shipping and returns. It uses the Katapull group as the running example. Katapull is a Dutch group that owns eight specialised software companies, one for each link of that chain. The tutorial covers why such a group exists, who its shared customers are and what makes connecting the links hard. It does not explain the internals of any single product, payment processing, accounting rules or how marketplaces themselves operate. Group facts come from public Katapull and brand websites as of late 2026. Characters and numbers in the walkthrough are invented for teaching.

01

What you will learn

  • Explain the commerce chain from order to delivery to return, and which kind of software handles each step.

  • Tell apart an all-in-one system, a set of separate best-of-breed tools, and a group of specialised products that are connected to each other.

  • Describe who the shared customers of such a group are and why one merchant ends up using several of its products.

  • Explain why connecting products that belong to the same owner is still hard work.

  • Reason about where a competitor such as Shopify competes with the group and where it is a channel for it.

02

Start with the everyday need

Every company that sells physical goods has to do the same chain of work. It takes an order, checks that the goods are there, picks and packs them, hands the parcel to a carrier, sends an invoice and sometimes takes the goods back. For a small webshop this may be one person with a laptop. For a wholesaler with thousands of products, several sales channels and hundreds of orders a day, every link in the chain is run by software.

The Katapull Commerce Hub is a Dutch group of eight software companies, each specialised in one link. Highbiza builds webshops. EffectConnect connects merchants to online marketplaces such as bol and Amazon. Vendit runs tills and stock in physical stores. Logic4 is the central business administration for trading companies. Transus exchanges electronic order and invoice documents with retailers. Picqer runs warehouses. DeliveryMatch books carriers and prints shipping labels. Returnista handles returns. The group says it serves more than 4,500 customers and 27,500 users.

Many merchants need more than one of these links, so the group's customers overlap. A wholesaler may run its administration in Logic4, sell through a Highbiza webshop and on marketplaces through EffectConnect, and ship with DeliveryMatch. For that merchant the real question is not whether each product is good on its own. It is whether the products work together, so that an order typed in once flows all the way to the customer's door without anyone copying it by hand.

A familiar comparison

Think of a hospital. Reception, the laboratory, radiology, the pharmacy and billing are separate departments with their own specialists and systems. The patient experiences one hospital visit, and it only goes well if the patient file moves smoothly from department to department. A group of commerce software companies is like a set of departments, and the merchant is the patient who walks through all of them.

Where the comparison stops

A hospital has one management and one patient file by design. The Katapull companies were independent firms that were bought one by one between 2025 and 2026. Each still has its own product, customers and way of storing data. They also sell separately: a merchant can buy only DeliveryMatch and use a competitor for everything else. The connections between them are built pair by pair and are uneven, so the hub is closer to a group of neighbouring clinics that agreed to share files than to one hospital.

03

The people involved

The merchant's owner or director

Runs a wholesale company, webshop, brand or store chain and decides which software to buy. Pays the monthly subscriptions.

What matters to them

Growing sales without hiring more back-office staff, a chain that does not break at busy times, and not paying twice for overlapping tools.

E-commerce and channel manager

Looks after online sales on the merchant's own webshop and on marketplaces. Works daily in the shop, the marketplace tool, the delivery options and the returns figures.

What matters to them

Conversion, winning the featured offer on marketplaces, correct product information everywhere, and returns that do not destroy margin.

Warehouse and logistics lead

Makes sure orders from every channel are picked, packed, shipped and, when they come back, put back on the shelf.

What matters to them

Shipping on time and complete, stock figures that are true, labels that print without manual work, and returns that arrive announced.

IT and finance staff

Keep the systems connected and the books right. In a mid-sized merchant this is often one or two people, not a department.

What matters to them

Few, stable connections, passing customers' security checks, electronic invoices that are accepted first time, and month-end without matching spreadsheets.

Specialty retailers, suppliers and fulfilment companies

Other kinds of shared customer. A bike or fashion store sells in-store and online. A brand supplies retailers that demand electronic documents. A fulfilment company (often called a 3PL, a third-party logistics provider) runs warehouses for many webshops at once.

What matters to them

Each has a different centre of gravity: the store till for the retailer, retailer requirements for the supplier, and fast onboarding and accurate billing of clients for the 3PL.

Buyers: shoppers and trade customers

The people who actually order the goods. They never see the software brands, but feel every link.

What matters to them

Correct stock information, delivery when and where it suits them, agreed prices for business buyers, and easy returns.

Software partners and competitors

Implementation partners and other software vendors that build on, resell or compete with the group's products. Examples are ERP consultants, Shopify, and all-in-one suites such as JTL or Odoo.

What matters to them

Partners want stable connections and a share of revenue. Competitors want to replace several links at once, or to be the centre the others plug into.

04

The key concepts

Sales channel

A place where a buyer can order: the merchant's own webshop, a marketplace run by someone else, a physical store, or a direct electronic link with a business customer.

For example

A bike-parts wholesaler sells to workshops through its own B2B webshop, to consumers on bol and Amazon, and to two store chains through electronic orders.

Why it matters

Every extra channel brings extra orders, but also another place where product information, prices and stock must be kept correct.

System of record (ERP)

The one system whose data counts as the truth for products, prices, customers, orders and money. In trading companies this is often an ERP (enterprise resource planning) system, such as Logic4. For a store-led business it is the store system, such as Vendit. Other tools copy from it and report back to it.

For example

If the webshop says a product costs 20 euros and the ERP says 22, the ERP wins and the webshop must be corrected.

Why it matters

Most integration trouble comes from two systems both believing they are the truth. Choosing the system of record decides which way data should flow.

Marketplace and featured offer (buy box)

A marketplace is a large shop where many merchants sell the same products next to each other. When several merchants offer the same item, the marketplace shows one offer by default, often called the buy box. Most sales go to that offer.

For example

Five sellers offer the same bike lock on bol. The one with a good price, fast delivery and good reviews gets the buy box and most of the orders.

Why it matters

Selling on marketplaces is a pricing and data game. Tools such as EffectConnect publish product data, adjust prices and pull orders back into the merchant's systems.

Warehouse management system (WMS)

Software that knows exactly where each product sits in the warehouse and directs the people who pick, pack and receive goods. Picqer is an example.

For example

The WMS groups twenty orders into one walking route through the aisles, tells the picker which shelf to go to, and checks with a barcode scan that the right item went into the box.

Why it matters

An ERP can record stock, but at higher volumes merchants need location-level control and efficient picking. That is why a merchant may outgrow the ERP's warehouse functions and add a WMS.

Multi-carrier shipping and the delivery promise

Instead of using one parcel company, the merchant connects many carriers through one shipping platform such as DeliveryMatch. The platform picks the best carrier per order and prints the label. The delivery promise is the delivery moment shown to the buyer at checkout.

For example

A small parcel goes with a cheap next-day carrier, a heavy box with a pallet carrier, and a consumer who chose evening delivery gets a carrier that can deliver in the evening.

Why it matters

A promise is not a delivery. The checkout can only promise what stock and carrier capacity can actually deliver, so the shipping tool needs to know about stock and warehouse cut-off times.

Returns, exchanges and store credit

A returns platform such as Returnista gives the shopper a branded page to register a return, print a label and choose a refund, an exchange for another size or store credit. It tells the warehouse what is coming back.

For example

A shopper returns shoes that are too small and chooses the next size up. The merchant keeps the sale, and the warehouse knows in advance that one pair is coming back.

Why it matters

In fashion, around three in ten items can come back. Turning refunds into exchanges and announcing returns before they arrive are major levers on profit.

EDI, despatch advice and SSCC labels

EDI (electronic data interchange) means companies send each other orders, delivery notices and invoices as structured electronic messages instead of PDFs or emails. A despatch advice tells the retailer exactly what is in a delivery. An SSCC (serial shipping container code) is a standard barcode label on each pallet or box. Transus runs such a network.

For example

A DIY chain sends a supplier an electronic order. The supplier ships two pallets with SSCC labels and sends a despatch advice, so the chain's distribution centre can scan the pallets and know their content without opening them.

Why it matters

Large retailers often refuse deliveries without correct EDI messages and labels. For suppliers this is not optional, and it ties the warehouse and the administration to the retailer's systems.

Fulfilment company (3PL)

A logistics firm that stores and ships goods on behalf of many webshops. The webshop owns the stock, and the 3PL does the warehouse work and charges per activity.

For example

A growing cosmetics brand moves its stock to a 3PL. The brand still sells on its own shop and marketplaces, but the 3PL picks, packs, ships and processes returns, and the brand watches it all through a portal.

Why it matters

A 3PL is a shared customer with many clients of its own. It needs warehouse, shipping and returns software that keeps every client separate and bills them correctly.

Integration (connector)

A piece of software that moves data automatically between two systems, for example orders from a marketplace tool into the ERP, or shipment numbers from the shipping platform back to the webshop. A productised integration is built once by the vendor and switched on by each customer. A custom integration is built for one customer.

For example

Vendit's marketplace module sends store stock to EffectConnect and receives marketplace orders back, without anyone exporting files.

Why it matters

The value of a group of products depends on these connections. Some are documented and used by many customers. Others exist only as a partner listing on a website, or are still plans.

All-in-one, best-of-breed and connected hub

Three ways to equip the chain. All-in-one means one product does everything at a basic level. Best-of-breed means the merchant picks the best separate tool for each link and connects them. A connected hub is a family of specialised products from one group with ready-made connections between them.

For example

JTL in Germany sells an all-in-one suite. A Shopify merchant who adds separate shipping and returns apps is going best-of-breed. A Logic4 customer who adds EffectConnect and DeliveryMatch through their existing connections is using the hub.

Why it matters

Each choice moves the hard work somewhere else: all-in-one compromises on depth, best-of-breed on connection work, and a hub on how deep its internal connections really are.

05

A little history

These turning points come from the Dutch and Belgian market and the histories of the Katapull brands. Other countries followed similar steps on different timetables.

  1. Late 1990s to 2000s

    Trading companies moved their administration from paper and stand-alone accounting packages to business software that also ran the webshop. Logic4, for example, traces its roots to 1998 and was founded in 2003. Highbiza's predecessor started building webshops more than twenty years ago.

    What this helps explain

    Many mid-sized merchants still expect one system to do everything, and judge newer specialised tools against that expectation.

    Source for Late 1990s to 2000s

  2. 2011 onwards

    Large Dutch shops such as bol opened their platforms to outside sellers. Specialised connectors appeared so that merchants could list products and receive orders without typing them over. EffectConnect says it was the first integrator connected to bol, in 2011.

    What this helps explain

    Marketplaces became a channel to manage like any other, with their own data rules, fees and pricing competition.

    Source for 2011 onwards

  3. 2010s

    Online volumes grew, and merchants started using many carriers and promising delivery moments at checkout. Shipping platforms such as DeliveryMatch (founded 2013) connected webshops and ERPs to hundreds of carriers.

    What this helps explain

    Shipping became a choice made per order, and the checkout became part of logistics.

    Source for 2010s

  4. 2020 to 2022

    During the COVID-19 period, online shopping and returns grew sharply. Dedicated returns platforms such as Returnista grew quickly. Returns turned from a back-room cost into something merchants actively manage with portals, exchanges and data.

    What this helps explain

    Returns now sit at the end of the chain and feed data back to buying, product content and warehouse planning.

    Source for 2020 to 2022

  5. 2025 to 2026

    Investor-backed Katapull brought Logic4, DeliveryMatch, EffectConnect, Highbiza and Vendit together in March 2025. Returnista joined in February 2026 and the warehouse software Picqer in October 2026. Transus is also listed as part of the hub.

    What this helps explain

    Specialised firms that had grown up as partners became sister companies. The question shifted from whether they can connect to how deep and reliable the connections are for shared customers.

    Source for 2025 to 2026

  6. 2027 to 2028 (announced)

    Germany is making electronic invoices mandatory for business-to-business sales: from 2027 for companies above a turnover threshold, and from 2028 for all.

    What this helps explain

    Electronic trade documents move from 'demanded by big retailers' to 'required by law', pulling EDI and e-invoicing into ordinary merchants' chains.

    Source for 2027 to 2028 (announced)

06

Follow one complete example

One working day at a bike-parts wholesaler (fictional)

Velomax is an invented wholesaler of bicycle parts near Utrecht with 40 employees. All names and numbers here are made up. It runs its administration in Logic4 and its B2B webshop on Highbiza. It sells on bol and Amazon through EffectConnect, ships with DeliveryMatch and recently moved its warehouse to Picqer. It uses Returnista for consumer returns, and Transus for one store chain that orders electronically. Anna is the e-commerce manager, Ruben runs the warehouse and Femke does finance and IT.

  1. Orders arrive from four directions

    By 9:00 Velomax has 180 orders. A bike workshop ordered brake pads in the Highbiza webshop at its agreed trade price. Consumers ordered on bol and Amazon, and EffectConnect pulled those orders in. A store chain sent an electronic order through Transus. All of them land in Logic4, the system of record, which books them and reserves stock.

    Why this step matters

    No one types an order over. Every channel has its own format, and the connections translate it into one way of recording an order.

    When things go differently

    If one channel has the wrong price or VAT setting, the error is copied automatically into every order from that channel.

  2. The warehouse picks and packs

    Logic4 passes the orders to Picqer. Picqer groups them into picking routes. Ruben's team scans every item, and Picqer checks that each box is complete.

    Why this step matters

    Velomax moved to Picqer because Logic4's warehouse functions were not enough at its volume. The handover between the two is one of the links the group is still building, so Femke checks it daily.

    When things go differently

    When two systems both record stock, they can disagree. Velomax decided that Picqer holds the stock per location and Logic4 holds the stock per product for sales.

  3. The right carrier for every box

    When a box is closed, Picqer asks DeliveryMatch for a label. Parcels for consumers go with a next-day carrier. The store chain's two pallets go with a pallet carrier. For those pallets, Transus sends the chain a despatch advice and Velomax prints SSCC labels.

    Why this step matters

    The carrier choice is automatic, but it must match what was promised at checkout and what the retailer demands at its distribution centre.

  4. Tracking flows back

    DeliveryMatch sends tracking numbers back. EffectConnect forwards them to bol and Amazon, and the webshop shows them to the workshop. Marketplaces judge sellers partly on how quickly tracking appears.

    Why this step matters

    Information has to travel back up the chain as well as down it. A late tracking number can cost the buy box next week.

  5. The exception: a product sold twice

    At 14:00 the last 3 units of a popular bike lock sell on Amazon at the same moment as a webshop order for 2. For a few minutes the stock figure shown on Amazon had not caught up with the webshop sale. Velomax now has 5 orders for 3 locks. Anna cancels one Amazon order and apologises. The cancellation counts against Velomax's seller rating.

    Why this step matters

    This is the classic problem of a chain: every link works, but the delay between links causes the failure. Shorter sync times and safety buffers on marketplaces reduce it but never remove it.

    When things go differently

    Overselling on marketplaces is penalised far more heavily than on your own webshop.

  6. A return comes back

    A consumer who bought a saddle on Velomax's own consumer shop registers a return in Returnista and picks a different model instead of a refund. Returnista creates a return label through DeliveryMatch and announces the return to Picqer. Two days later Ruben's team scans the parcel, Picqer puts the saddle back in stock, and the exchange ships.

    Why this step matters

    Three platforms work together here through connections that already exist. The sale is kept, and the warehouse knew what was coming.

    When things go differently

    Whether the refund or exchange is booked correctly in Logic4 depends on a connection that is still planned. Femke reconciles it by hand at month end.

  7. Month end

    Femke closes the books in Logic4. She sends electronic invoices to the store chain through Transus, checks carrier invoices against DeliveryMatch bookings, and compares marketplace fees with sales.

    Why this step matters

    Finance is where every gap in the chain shows up as a mismatch that someone has to explain.

The outcome

Velomax shipped almost every order on time with one small warehouse team and four sales channels. The one oversell and the hand-booked refunds show where the chain is still weak: in the connections between the platforms, not inside any one of them. For Velomax, success means more orders per employee and fewer things checked by hand. For the software group, it means Velomax keeps using several of its products and adds the next one.

07

Value, money, and incentives

For the merchant: less manual work and one point of contact

The main benefit is that data moves by itself from order to delivery to invoice. Fewer people are needed per order, and there are fewer errors to fix. A group of sister companies also promises that when something breaks between two products, the merchant does not get sent back and forth between two unrelated vendors.

For example

When Velomax added DeliveryMatch to Logic4, it stopped printing labels in each carrier's own website.

For the group: customers who use more products stay longer

The companies earn monthly subscriptions, often plus fees per order or shipment. A merchant using one product can switch fairly easily. A merchant whose shop, marketplaces, warehouse and shipping all depend on connected products rarely leaves. So the group's key goal is to raise the number of its products each customer uses and to keep the connections reliable.

For example

A Logic4 customer who later adds Picqer and Returnista becomes much harder for a competitor to win, because the competitor would have to replace three connected products.

Costs that do not show on the price list

Building and maintaining connections costs a lot. Every product changes, every marketplace and carrier updates its rules, and a connection that worked last year can break at the busiest moment. Somebody has to pay for that maintenance, through subscriptions, implementation fees or the merchant's own staff.

For example

A marketplace changes its required product fields before the holiday season. EffectConnect must update its mapping, and merchants must fill in the missing data.

Where interests pull apart

Each company still has its own targets and customers. Some products overlap: Logic4 has its own webshop and till, which overlap with Highbiza and Vendit, and Picqer prints labels, which overlaps with DeliveryMatch. Partners such as Shopify and ERP consultants bring customers but would prefer to be the centre themselves. The merchant wants freedom of choice. The group prefers that the merchant chooses more of its own products.

For example

A retailer on Vendit asks whether to build its webshop in Vendit's own shop or in Highbiza. Both are Katapull products, and the honest answer depends on the retailer, not on which brand gets the revenue.

08

What makes this domain difficult

Connections of very different depth

The companies were built separately and bought one by one, each with its own data model. Some pairs have worked together for years. Others exist only as a logo on a partner page, and some are still plans. From the outside they all look like 'connected'.

The competing needs

Building every connection deeply would take years of engineering from every brand. Promising them all now raises expectations the products cannot yet meet.

In practice

Picqer and Returnista have a documented two-way integration. A connection between Logic4 and Picqer is positioned by the group but not yet published.

How people respond, and its limits

Publish which connections exist and how deep they are, and build the ones that the most shared customers need first. The limitation is that customers with unusual combinations still fall in the gaps.

Who holds the truth

Stock, prices and order status are stored in several products at once. When they disagree, the chain needs a clear rule about which system wins, and that rule differs per customer.

The competing needs

Letting one system own everything is simple but makes the others less useful. Letting each own its piece needs careful rules and fast synchronisation.

In practice

Velomax's oversold bike lock happened because the marketplace saw old stock for a few minutes.

How people respond, and its limits

Decide the system of record per type of data, sync changes in near real time, and keep safety buffers on channels with heavy penalties. Buffers cost some sales on quiet days.

Overlap between sister products

Several products grew to cover their neighbours' ground before they joined the group. Customers and salespeople must choose between two Katapull answers to the same need.

The competing needs

Retiring an overlapping function upsets the customers who use it. Keeping both confuses buyers and splits development effort.

In practice

Logic4 phased out its own bol.com connection in favour of EffectConnect. EffectConnect's Fitwinkel case describes one customer making that move.

How people respond, and its limits

Position each product for a clear type of customer and offer guided migration. This works best when the replacement is clearly better for that customer.

Competitors that are also channels

Some rivals compete for one part of the chain while hosting another. Shopify competes with Highbiza, Vendit and Logic4 for the shop and the system of record. Yet DeliveryMatch, Returnista, Picqer and EffectConnect all sell apps or connectors into Shopify stores. All-in-one suites such as JTL or Odoo try to replace several links at once.

The competing needs

Fighting a host platform closes a large market for the fulfilment products. Embracing it weakens the case for the group's own shops and ERP.

In practice

A Shopify brand can use four Katapull products and never touch Logic4, Vendit or Highbiza.

How people respond, and its limits

Treat merchants that run on Shopify or another ERP as a segment in their own right, while competing on depth for merchants who want a Katapull core. The risk is a mixed message to the market.

Peaks and reliability

Merchants earn a large share of their year in a few weeks, such as Black Friday and December. That is exactly when order volumes, marketplace traffic and carrier load all spike, and a failure in any connection stops the chain.

The competing needs

Building for peak capacity costs money all year. Building for the average risks failing customers when it matters most.

In practice

If the connection that sends shipping labels to the warehouse fails on a peak day, hundreds of packed boxes wait on the floor.

How people respond, and its limits

Monitor connection health, test for peak volumes in advance and offer fallback procedures. Monitoring finds problems faster but does not prevent them.

09

Common misunderstandings

If one company owns all the products, they are automatically one integrated system.

A better way to understand it

Shared ownership makes it easier to agree on connections, but every product still has its own data and code. Connections must be built and maintained pair by pair, and their depth varies a lot.

A merchant buys the whole hub at once.

A better way to understand it

Most merchants start with one product and add others over years, often when they hit a limit, such as a warehouse outgrowing its software or a retailer demanding EDI. Each product is still sold and priced on its own.

Shopify is simply a competitor of the group.

A better way to understand it

It competes for the webshop, till and central system, but several of the group's fulfilment and marketplace products sell into Shopify stores. It is a competitor and a channel at the same time.

The hardest part is inside each product.

A better way to understand it

Each product handles its own job reasonably well. Most failures that merchants notice happen between products: delays, mismatched data, and missing links.

10

The ideas to take with you

  • Selling physical goods is a chain of work: channel, system of record, trade documents, warehouse, shipping and returns. Each link can be run by different software.

  • A group like Katapull owns a specialised product for each link. Its value to a merchant depends on how well those products connect, not only on how good each one is.

  • Shared customers include trading companies, specialty retailers, suppliers to retail, fulfilment companies, Shopify-based brands and software partners. Each uses a different combination of products.

  • Connections between sister products differ in depth: some are proven, some are only listed, some are plans. That gap is where merchants feel friction.

  • Competitors come in three shapes: all-in-one suites, best-of-breed tools, and platforms such as Shopify that compete for the centre while hosting the rest.

11

Try it for yourself

Pause and think through each situation, then open the answer.

1. Velomax oversold a bike lock even though every product worked correctly. What caused it, and why is it worse on a marketplace than on the own webshop?

Reveal answer to question 1

A short delay in passing the stock change to Amazon. Marketplaces penalise cancellations in the seller's rating.

The reasoning

The failure sat between systems, not inside one. Because the marketplace controls who gets the buy box, a cancellation there costs future sales, not just one order.

2. A fashion brand runs its shop on Shopify and has no plans to change. Which kinds of Katapull product could it still use?

Reveal answer to question 2

Marketplace connection (EffectConnect), warehouse (Picqer), shipping (DeliveryMatch) and returns (Returnista).

The reasoning

These products sell apps or connectors into Shopify stores. The brand would not need the group's shop, till or central administration, because Shopify plays that role.

3. Why might a merchant that already uses Logic4 add Picqer, when Logic4 also records stock?

Reveal answer to question 3

Because at higher volumes the warehouse needs location-level control and efficient picking that a dedicated warehouse system provides.

The reasoning

Recording how much stock exists is different from directing people around a warehouse. The trade-off is a new connection between two systems that both hold stock, which needs a clear rule about which one is the truth.

4. A supplier of garden tools must deliver pallets to a DIY chain. Which three things from the tutorial must line up for the delivery to be accepted?

Reveal answer to question 4

The electronic order and despatch advice (Transus), correct SSCC labels on the pallets, and a pallet carrier booked for the delivery (DeliveryMatch).

The reasoning

Large retailers check deliveries against the electronic messages. The warehouse, the trade-document network and the carrier all play a part, so a gap in any of them leads to a rejected delivery.

5. From the group's point of view, why does a customer using three connected products matter more than three customers using one product each?

Reveal answer to question 5

Connected customers are much less likely to leave, and they show the group's real advantage over separate tools.

The reasoning

To win such a customer, a competitor would have to replace several connected products at once. That makes the number of products per customer and the reliability of the connections the group's key measures.

12

Questions to ask a practitioner

  • Which combinations of products do your shared customers use most, and which connections between them are proven in daily use?

  • When two of your products both hold stock or prices, which one is the truth, and who decides?

  • How do you advise a customer when two sister products cover the same need?

  • What happens to the connections on the busiest day of the year, and how quickly do you know when one fails?

  • How do you treat customers whose central system is Shopify, Exact or Business Central rather than one of your own?
