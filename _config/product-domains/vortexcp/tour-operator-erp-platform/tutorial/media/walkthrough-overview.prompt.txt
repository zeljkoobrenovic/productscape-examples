Use case: scientific-educational
Asset type: one illustration in a plain-language product-domain tutorial for adults.
Domain: From a travel idea to a trip that works
Reader: Someone arriving from another field, with no assumed knowledge of travel businesses, accounting, or business software.
Scope: An introduction to the work of tour operators and the software that connects planning, sales, suppliers, travellers, and finance. Examples reflect group and tailor-made travel in a European setting. Legal duties depend on the arrangement, country, and applicable dates. This is not a guide to running an airline or choosing a holiday. Nora, Coast & Village Travel, the trip, and all prices and contract terms are fictional.
Subject: Nora organizes a six-person weekend
Setup: This fictional three-day trip includes minibus travel, two hotel nights, and a guided walk. Six adults agree to share three twin rooms. The €150 price per person and all supplier amounts are teaching figures, not real travel prices. Assume taxes and other costs are excluded throughout the example.
Step 1: Describe the trip and who it suits. Nora records the route, dates, inclusions, room arrangements, meeting points, and practical requirements. She makes the offer clear enough for travellers to judge whether it suits them. Why: Good preparation starts with a trip people can actually take, including any relevant access or mobility requirements. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 2: Arrange supply and calculate the offer. She checks suitable transport, three twin rooms for both nights, and a guide. She records supplier terms and deadlines. Expected direct costs are €700 against €900 sales if all six places sell. Why: Capacity, price, and commitments must fit the same departure. Nora also considers the effect of selling fewer places. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 3: Record bookings and confirm each component. The six travellers accept the offer. Nora records their arrangements and supplier replies, resolving pending items before treating the trip as secured. Finance matches €270 of customer deposits to bookings and plans the €350 of supplier advances. Why: Customer commitments, supplier commitments, and payments remain visible as distinct facts. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 4: Prepare the people delivering the trip. Nora checks guest details and agreed room sharing, then prepares itineraries, rooming information, pickup details, and guide instructions. Each recipient gets the information needed for their role. Why: A confirmed hotel still needs to know whom to expect. Preparing different documents from shared records reduces contradictions. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 5: Handle a hotel problem. The hotel can no longer provide the rooms. Nora finds a suitable alternative, explains the change and available options, and checks the applicable obligations. In this case all travellers accept, and the operator keeps their price unchanged. Why: Assume the original hotel payment is fully refunded and the replacement costs €360. Direct costs rise to €760, leaving €140 trip margin. Possible complication: Nora updates the hotel details, rooming instructions, driver destination, guide plan, customer itinerary, and financial records. Changing only the customer email would leave other people working from the old plan.
Step 6: Deliver, check, and close the trip. The group travels and the team handles any questions. Afterwards, finance matches final supplier bills, refunds, and customer payments to the records. Nora reviews traveller feedback and the departure's actual costs. Why: The group returning home and the accounts being settled are separate milestones. Actual results inform the next departure. Possible complication: None specified.
Outcome: The travellers complete the agreed trip with the replacement hotel. Everyone delivering it receives the updated plan, and the operator can explain the €140 margin before other costs. The software supports these handoffs; Nora and her colleagues make and check the decisions.

Teaching scene:
A spare six-panel editorial storyboard with only SIX text labels. Strict 3-column by 2-row grid; one large title per panel: '1 Plan', '2 Arrange supply', '3 Confirm', '4 Prepare', '5 Handle change', '6 Deliver and settle'. Absolutely no other words, letters, names, dates, amounts or numbers anywhere: screens and documents contain only abstract lines, colored shapes and icons. No lists of passenger names. No captions inside panels. Consistent Nora has short curly dark hair and a teal jacket. 1: Nora and travellers examine a coastal route. 2: Nora considers separate hotel, minibus and walking-guide picture cards. 3: Nora checks two separate piles of customer and supplier documents, with simple person and hotel icons. 4: Nora gives different documents to a hotel worker and minibus driver. 5: Nora phones while replacing an unavailable hotel picture with another, simple amber question mark. 6: travellers enjoy the coastal village, with a separate small finance scene of a person matching paper records. Use big human scenes and white space, not dense process diagrams. No arrows between panels. All six headings must be very legible at thumbnail size.

Style and composition:
- Aspect ratio 16:9, generous margins, bright warm-white background.
- A polished adult educational illustration: flat editorial drawing, crisp navy outlines,
  restrained teal, blue, and warm amber accents, subtle texture, very little shading.
- Make the concept understandable at the width of a reading column and on a phone.
- Use a small number of recognizable objects and people. Keep relationships spatially clear.
- Use an inclusive cast without occupational or cultural stereotypes.
- Short everyday labels are welcome where they teach the distinction. Use only labels
  explicitly requested in the teaching scene; otherwise keep the image free of text.
- Arrows must have a clear meaning and direction; do not connect unrelated comparison
  panels as though they are required stages. Do not imply certainty, causality, proportions,
  cash movements, or guaranteed outcomes that the explanation does not support.
- Keep all important content inside the frame. No headline, watermark, brand logo,
  decorative charts, invented statistics, jargon, tiny writing, photorealism, or 3D rendering.
- Explain the idea visually. The surrounding tutorial provides its full definition and caveats.
