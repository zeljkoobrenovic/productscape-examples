A beginner's guide

Transfer pricing: how companies explain prices inside a group

About 18 min to readUpdated 2026-09-08

Who this is for

A curious adult arriving from another field. No background in tax, accounting, law, or business software is assumed.

What this guide covers

An introduction to cross-border transfer pricing and the people, records, and software behind its annual documentation cycle. It uses the common framework of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), with an EU example in the history. Countries set their own legal requirements; OECD guidance is not a worldwide filing law. Detailed tax calculations, customs duties, and country-specific filing instructions are outside this guide. Luma, its people, countries A and B, and all amounts are fictional teaching examples.

01

What you will learn

  • Explain why a price between two companies in one group matters for tax.

  • Connect a price explanation to the work, resources, and risks of each company.

  • Distinguish the main documentation packages and follow a complete preparation cycle.

  • Explain where software helps and why consistent documents still need human review.

02

Start with the everyday need

Imagine a lamp made in one country and sold in another. The factory and the local seller belong to the same business group, but they are separate companies. The factory charges the seller for the lamp. That internal charge is a transfer price. Similar charges arise when related companies provide services, lend money, or let each other use a brand or invention.

A higher purchase price leaves less profit with the seller and more with the factory. Tax authorities therefore want to understand how the group chose its terms. Transfer pricing compliance means applying the relevant rules and keeping a supportable explanation of what happened. A compliance platform helps people collect facts, review calculations, prepare reports, and track obligations.

A familiar comparison

Think of two separately run stalls at a fair: one bakes cakes and another sells them. The price between the stalls affects how much each earns, even though the customer sees only the final price.

Where the comparison stops

Independent stall owners can bargain for themselves. Companies with common owners may follow a group decision instead. Separate countries also apply their own tax rules. A shared owner does not make the companies' separate tax responsibilities disappear.

03

The people involved

Business and local finance teams

Run the factory or selling company and record sales, expenses, and internal charges. They know what actually happened.

What matters to them

Accurate accounts, manageable requests, and descriptions that reflect their work. In our example, Leo manages the selling company's finances.

The group's tax team

Coordinates the explanation across companies, obtains expert review, and plans the reporting work. Sofia leads this work at fictional Luma.

What matters to them

Tax treatment the group can explain, deadlines, and consistency across countries. The group funds the software and any outside help.

Legal colleagues and company decision-makers

Maintain company and contract records and approve decisions within their responsibilities.

What matters to them

Agreements that reflect real arrangements and a clear record of who authorized changes.

Tax advisers and platform providers

Supply specialist analysis, software, or document preparation. One provider may offer both software and services.

What matters to them

Clear scope, reliable inputs, review time, and fees that cover the work. Outsourcing preparation still leaves the company with responsibilities.

Tax authorities

Administer local tax rules, assess risk, and examine selected cases. Each country's authority has its own role.

What matters to them

Correct taxable profit and useful explanations. Receiving a report does not mean accepting every position in it.

04

The key concepts

Related companies and transfer prices

Related companies are connected through ownership or control; the precise legal test varies. A transfer price is the charge for something one supplies to another. Profit, in our simplified example, means sales income minus the included costs, before tax.

For example

Luma's factory spends €60 making a lamp and charges its related seller €80. The seller sells it to a customer for €100 and incurs €10 of selling costs. Factory profit is €20; seller profit is €10. If the internal price were €90, those profits would become €30 and €0. Combined profit stays €30. This assumes the lamp is sold and ignores all other costs and taxes.

Why it matters

Changing the internal price changes where profit appears without changing the customer's purchase. Neither illustrative price has been established as acceptable for tax.

The arm's length principle

Ask what independent businesses would have agreed in comparable circumstances. 'Arm's length' describes independence, not physical distance. The question includes payment terms and responsibilities as well as the price.

For example

An unrelated seller buying one lamp for immediate payment may be a poor comparison for Luma's seller buying thousands, storing stock, and paying later.

Why it matters

The test is neither 'whatever the group prefers' nor automatically the retail price. A sound analysis may support a range of results rather than one uniquely correct number.

Functions, assets, and risks

These three words organize a factual investigation, often called a functional analysis. Functions are the work people do; assets are resources they use, including equipment and valuable knowledge; risks are uncertainties that can bring gains or losses. Contracts matter alongside actual decisions, conduct, and the financial ability to bear a risk.

For example

Who designs Luma's lamps? Who decides how much stock to hold? Who authorizes warranty repairs and bears their cost? A contract alone cannot answer whether daily practice follows that arrangement.

Why it matters

Two companies called 'distributors' may do very different work. Their names or contract labels alone do not establish the profit each should earn.

Comparables and pricing methods

Comparables are relevant observations about independent businesses or transactions. A pricing method is a structured way to use such information: for example, comparing transaction prices, adding an appropriate profit to costs, or examining profit relative to sales. That last relationship is a profit margin. Method choice depends on the facts and reliable information available.

For example

Sofia checks whether other sellers perform similar work in similar markets. A business developing its own distinctive lamp designs may be unsuitable for comparison with a seller that only distributes lamps.

Why it matters

A database supplies candidates, not an automatic answer. The team must explain its selection and important differences. There is no universal percentage to apply to every internal charge.

Policy, actual results, and reconciliation

A policy describes the intended approach to internal charges. Actual results are what the accounts record. Reconciliation means checking how records fit together and explaining differences. Some cases require an adjustment, but correcting a data error and changing a price are different actions.

For example

One spreadsheet shows Luma's lamp purchase at €90 while the invoice and accounts show €80. Sofia first investigates the mismatch. Editing the invoice merely to match the spreadsheet would conceal the problem.

Why it matters

A sensible policy can be implemented incorrectly. Before considering a pricing adjustment, the team needs reliable figures and a reason that fits the facts and applicable rules.

Master file, local file, and country-by-country report

The OECD documentation framework contains three views. A master file explains the group as a whole. A local file examines relevant transactions of a local company, including the analysis and financial information. A country-by-country report summarizes information such as revenue, profit, tax, and activity by country for groups within its scope. It is often shortened to CbCR.

For example

Think of a group portrait, a close-up of Luma's seller, and a country summary table. They answer different questions. Local law determines which documents are required, their contents, and whether they are filed routinely or kept ready for a request.

Why it matters

CbCR helps authorities identify risks; it does not prove an individual price is right. Public country-by-country reporting, such as the EU regime, is a separate disclosure obligation with its own rules.

An audit trail

An audit trail connects a reported conclusion to its inputs, calculations, versions, and reviewers. Here, an audit means an examination by a tax authority. The trail helps someone reconstruct the work later; it does not guarantee the authority will agree.

For example

For Luma's seller, Sofia can retrieve the source accounts, the comparison study, the explanation of a difference, and the approved version of the report.

Why it matters

A polished document is much more useful when its statements can be checked. Software can preserve the chain, while people remain responsible for the reasoning and review.

05

A little history

These selected OECD and EU milestones explain today's shared vocabulary and reporting work. They are not a complete global history, and countries have followed different paths. The practical lessons are teaching interpretations.

  1. 1979–1995: building shared guidance

    The OECD's 1995 Transfer Pricing Guidelines built on its 1979 report on transfer pricing and multinational enterprises.

    What this helps explain

    International business needed ways to discuss internal prices across separate tax systems. Shared guidance provided a common reference without replacing national law.

    Source for 1979–1995: building shared guidance

  2. 2015: documentation gains three connected views

    The OECD/G20 project on Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) addressed ways businesses reduce or shift taxable profits. Its Action 13 report set out the master file, local file, and country-by-country reporting framework.

    What this helps explain

    The resulting reporting work connects local transactions with the wider group. Reusing consistent information across documents becomes valuable.

    Source for 2015: documentation gains three connected views

  3. 2021: the EU adds a public reporting regime

    The EU's public country-by-country reporting directive entered into force in December 2021, introducing disclosure requirements for businesses within its scope.

    What this helps explain

    Some information now has a wider audience than tax authorities. Teams must distinguish public reporting from tax-authority reporting, including their separate scope and timing.

    Source for 2021: the EU adds a public reporting regime

06

Follow one complete example

Sofia prepares Luma's annual explanation

Follow one fictional lamp transaction within a fictional multinational group. Luma Manufacturing in country A supplies Luma Sales in country B. Sofia coordinates tax work; Leo supplies the seller's facts and accounts. The €60 cost, €80 internal price, €100 customer price, and €10 selling cost illustrate arithmetic only, not a benchmark or recommended price.

  1. Map the work and the obligations

    Sofia identifies the companies, transaction, reporting year, and responsible people. With local specialists, she checks which reports and notifications apply, their deadlines, and whether they must be submitted or retained.

    Why this step matters

    The team's task list comes from applicable requirements. Having a template does not make that document mandatory.

  2. Understand the business behind the contract

    Leo explains ordering, storage, sales, and repairs. Legal colleagues provide the agreement. Sofia records who performs the work and makes decisions, checking the description with the people involved.

    Why this step matters

    The price analysis needs an accurate business story before it needs polished wording.

  3. Develop and explain the analysis

    Sofia and an adviser choose to examine seller profit relative to sales, recording why that method and the selected comparisons suit their assumed facts. Here €10 from €100 of sales is a 10% margin. Arithmetic alone does not establish whether that result is supportable.

    Why this step matters

    A calculation can be correct even when the comparison or assumptions behind it are unsuitable.

  4. Check actual results and investigate a change

    Leo connects the transaction figures to the accounts and explains differences. He also reports that the seller began authorizing and paying for warranty repairs during the year, while the earlier description assigned those responsibilities to the factory.

    Why this step matters

    Sofia reopens the business description and analysis with the adviser instead of copying the old conclusion.

    When things go differently

    The change may affect the analysis, but it does not by itself dictate a new price. The team checks the facts, timing, contracts, and relevant rules before deciding what to update.

  5. Prepare, localize, and review the documents

    The team updates the repair description and affected analysis, then the platform assembles drafts. Sofia checks shared statements, Leo checks numbers, and local specialists check content and language. Reviewers resolve open issues before approving final versions.

    Why this step matters

    A generated draft saves assembly work. It still needs checks that the explanation is accurate and locally suitable.

  6. Complete the obligations and keep the trail

    The responsible people submit what is required through the relevant channels, keep confirmations, and retain other required records. Sofia archives the supporting work and records changes to revisit next year.

    Why this step matters

    Completion includes evidence of submission where needed and the ability to answer later questions. An exported file alone proves neither.

The outcome

Luma has a reviewed explanation, traceable figures, and a record of completed obligations. The repair change is addressed rather than hidden in reused text. This case does not establish an acceptable real-world price or predict a tax authority's decision.

07

Value, money, and incentives

The group buys dependable preparation

Software subscriptions and specialist fees pay for coordination, reusable information, and reviewed documents. Time saved matters most when it also reduces missed obligations, unexplained differences, and repeated requests.

For example

Recording Luma's agreed business description once can prevent several local reports from telling conflicting stories.

The service still costs human attention

Major effort goes into collecting reliable data, evaluating comparisons, maintaining country knowledge, translation, and review. Providers may charge for access, document preparation, or advisory work.

For example

A fixed preparation fee can make costs predictable, but unusual transactions still need an agreed scope and enough expert time.

Participants judge success differently

Local teams want fewer interruptions; group teams want consistent answers; authorities need usable information. Their priorities can conflict when deadlines approach.

For example

A dashboard showing every draft as complete may look reassuring while Leo's repair question remains unresolved. Counting files alone misses that problem.

08

What makes this domain difficult

Finding useful comparisons

Independent businesses rarely match perfectly, especially when valuable designs, brands, or unusual responsibilities are involved.

The competing needs

Narrower selection can improve similarity while leaving less data to analyze.

In practice

Excluding every seller with a different product may leave Sofia with no useful observations.

How people respond, and its limits

Explain the economically important differences and use reliable adjustments where possible. Document uncertainty; a larger database cannot guarantee a close match.

Getting numbers and business descriptions to agree

Accounts, contracts, and operational knowledge come from different people and may describe different periods.

The competing needs

Early drafting helps meet deadlines, but premature finalization can preserve stale assumptions.

In practice

Luma's accounts include repair costs while its reused description still says the factory handles repairs.

How people respond, and its limits

Assign owners to facts and figures, reconcile totals, and flag changes for review. A complete upload cannot confirm that people described the business correctly.

Sharing information while meeting local requirements

Countries can differ in scope, content, language, deadlines, and submission rules. A shared framework does not remove these differences.

The competing needs

Common templates save effort; excessive uniformity can omit a local requirement.

In practice

Sofia may need to retain one country's document but actively submit another country's report.

How people respond, and its limits

Combine shared facts with reviewed local requirements and dated rule sources. Check changes before each cycle; translating a document alone does not make it locally suitable.

Resolving disagreement across countries

One authority may increase a company's taxable profit without the other country immediately granting a corresponding reduction. The same profit can then be taxed twice.

The competing needs

Seeking certainty requires time and specialist resources, while unresolved disputes consume attention and cash.

In practice

Country A increases Luma Manufacturing's taxable profit; relief for Luma Sales in country B is a separate matter.

How people respond, and its limits

Preserve the supporting analysis and assess available remedies. A treaty's mutual agreement procedure lets authorities work to resolve disputes; an advance pricing arrangement can establish an approach for future covered transactions. Access, conditions, and outcomes depend on the case.

Automating work without hiding weak reasoning

Software can reproduce an outdated assumption consistently across many files. Fluent AI text can make an unsupported explanation sound convincing.

The competing needs

More automation speeds assembly but increases the reach of an unnoticed error.

In practice

A drafting assistant repeats Luma's old repair arrangement in every translated report.

How people respond, and its limits

Keep source links, visible review status, and named reviewers for important claims. Restrict sensitive information to the people who need it. A technical validation pass checks only what its rules actually test.

09

Common misunderstandings

Transfer pricing means tax avoidance.

A better way to understand it

Related companies need terms for ordinary business transactions. Those terms can be misused, but the existence of an internal charge does not establish abuse.

A signed contract proves the price is acceptable.

A better way to understand it

It is part of the explanation. Actual conduct, relevant comparisons, and applicable rules still matter.

Every company must file all three documentation packages.

A better way to understand it

Requirements depend on the company, group, transaction, year, and local rules. The three packages are distinct views, not a universal filing checklist.

A loss or a filed report settles the question.

A better way to understand it

A loss can have ordinary business causes and deserves investigation. Filing establishes that something was submitted; it does not establish that its pricing analysis is correct.

10

The ideas to take with you

  • Internal prices affect where profit is recorded, so their explanation matters to more than one company and country.

  • Start with the real work and arrangements, then connect the method, figures, and documents.

  • Group, local, and country-summary views have different purposes and requirements.

  • A useful platform supports coordination and traceability; reviewed reasoning and completed obligations remain the goal.

11

Try it for yourself

Pause and think through each situation, then open the answer.

1. In the simplified lamp example, the internal price rises from €80 to €90. The final sale and other costs stay the same. What changes?

Reveal answer to question 1

Factory profit rises from €20 to €30, while seller profit falls from €10 to €0. Combined profit remains €30.

The reasoning

The internal sale is income for one company and a cost for the other. This calculation says nothing about which price is acceptable for tax.

2. The contract says the factory handles repairs, but Leo says the seller now decides and pays for them. Is updating the report's wording enough?

Reveal answer to question 2

No. Check the changed responsibilities, their timing, and their effect on the analysis with the appropriate reviewers.

The reasoning

A factual change can affect the comparison and conclusion. Better wording alone cannot resolve that question.

3. A country-by-country summary totals correctly. Does that establish that Luma's lamp price is appropriate?

Reveal answer to question 3

No. The summary helps identify risks; individual transactions still need their own supporting analysis.

The reasoning

A correct total answers a different question from whether a price reflects comparable independent arrangements.

4. The platform exports every final report. What must Sofia check before calling the cycle complete?

Reveal answer to question 4

That required reviews are complete, applicable submissions and notifications are made, and supporting records and confirmations are retained.

The reasoning

Creating an output file is one step in fulfilling an obligation. Some documents are submitted; others must be kept available.

12

Questions to ask a practitioner

  • Which transactions and business changes require the most judgment here?

  • Who can explain what the companies actually do, and who confirms the numbers?

  • How do you determine current obligations for each company and country?

  • Can a reviewer trace a report statement back to its source and approval?
