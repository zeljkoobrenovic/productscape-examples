A beginner's guide

Venture capital and private equity, from the beginning

About 20 min to readUpdated 2026-09-08

Who this is for

A curious reader coming from a different field. You do not need a finance background or experience running a company.

What this guide covers

An introduction to investing in businesses through venture, growth, and buyout funds, with software companies as everyday examples. It explains common arrangements rather than any particular investment firm's terms. Fund structures, investor access, and ownership rights vary by country and agreement. The people, businesses, and financial examples are fictional.

01

What you will learn

  • Explain how venture capital and buyouts help with different business needs.

  • Identify whose money is involved, who makes decisions, and who receives the money.

  • Follow an investment from fundraising to company ownership and an eventual sale.

  • Distinguish business progress, an estimated investment value, and money actually returned.

  • Recognize the main tensions around growth, control, debt, and timing.

02

Start with the everyday need

Imagine Maya has built software that helps repair businesses schedule their workers. Customers like it, but hiring a sales team costs money before those extra sales arrive. Elsewhere, an owner of an established software business wants to retire. These are different problems, yet both might be solved by bringing in a new business owner.

Venture capital and private equity involve investing money in return for a share of business ownership. Here, private generally means the ownership is not freely traded on a stock exchange. Investors hope their ownership becomes more valuable and can eventually be sold. They can also lose money. The work combines choosing businesses, agreeing on ownership, helping or overseeing them, and finding a way to return money to investors.

The effects reach beyond investors: companies may gain resources to grow, founders may sell part of what they built, and employees and customers may experience changes in priorities. Understanding the domain means following both the money and the decisions it enables.

A familiar comparison

Think of several people contributing money to help someone expand a neighborhood business. They appoint a representative to examine the plan, negotiate their share, and stay involved. The investors share the potential rewards and risks.

Where the comparison stops

Professional investing separates the investors, the investment organization, and the businesses they own. Detailed agreements govern their relationship. Investors may have to wait many years to get money back, and owning shares does not automatically mean running the business every day.

03

The people involved

The fund investors

Organizations such as pension funds, or other eligible investors, pool money in a fund: an arrangement for investing together. In a partnership structure, these investors are called limited partners, shortened to LPs.

What matters to them

Returns after costs, the possibility of losses, trustworthy reporting, and when money may come back.

The investment manager

Selects investments and oversees them for the fund. You may hear the managing side called the general partner, or GP; precise legal roles depend on the structure.

What matters to them

Finding worthwhile investments, supporting them, earning fees and a possible share of profits, and retaining investors' confidence.

Founders, selling owners, and company management

Founders may seek money for growth or sell ownership. Management runs the business. These can be the same people, but they need not be.

What matters to them

Resources, an acceptable price, decision-making freedom, and a workable relationship after the agreement is signed.

Lenders and specialist advisers

Lenders may supply borrowed money. Lawyers, accountants, and other specialists help examine and complete investments; fund administrators maintain records and reporting.

What matters to them

For lenders, repayment. For advisers and administrators, accurate work within their responsibilities. Neither role is the same as owning the business.

Employees and customers

Keep the business working and buying its services, although they may have little say in an ownership transaction.

What matters to them

Good jobs, dependable products, fair treatment, and continuity when ownership or strategy changes.

04

The key concepts

Venture capital, growth investment, and buyouts

A venture is a risky or uncertain project or business. Venture capital (VC) invests in young companies with high growth potential but also significant uncertainty. Growth investing supports more established companies that want to expand, while buyouts involve acquiring control of an existing business. These categories can overlap.

Private equity (PE) is a broad term that includes venture capital. However, in everyday industry use, PE often refers more specifically to buyout investing.

For example

Funding Maya to prove a young business model is a venture case. Funding expansion after it is established may be a growth case. Buying control from an owner who wants to leave is a buyout case.

Why it matters

Different needs call for different investors, expectations, and ownership arrangements. A company does not have to pass through all three stages.

The firm, the fund, and the portfolio

The firm is the investment organization. A fund is a separate arrangement that pools investors' money under agreed rules. The portfolio is the set of investments it holds.

For example

One firm may manage several funds raised at different times. Each fund has its own investors, commitments, and investments.

Why it matters

Money raised for a fund is not sales revenue earned by the investment firm.

A commitment and a capital call

A commitment is an investor's promise to provide an agreed amount. A capital call is a request to pay part of that commitment when the fund needs it, under its agreement.

For example

An investor commits €10 million. A €2 million call asks for part of that amount now; it does not mean the entire €10 million has already been spent.

Why it matters

A promise of money, cash received, and cash invested are different things. Calls can also fund agreed fees and expenses.

Equity and debt

Equity means ownership. Debt means borrowed money with repayment obligations. Equity owners receive what remains for them after obligations are met; lenders have different rights.

For example

A fund might buy shares using investors' money while a lender also finances the acquisition. Using borrowing to support a purchase is called leverage.

Why it matters

Debt can magnify both gains and losses for owners. Loan payments can strain a business even when its long-term idea is promising. Not every investment uses debt.

New shares versus existing shares

When a company issues new shares, the payment goes to the company. When an owner sells existing shares, the payment goes to that seller. A transaction can combine both.

For example

Maya's business can receive money to hire people, or Maya can personally receive money for selling ownership. Those are different uses of an investment.

Why it matters

A headline saying a company received investment does not tell you how much cash entered its bank account.

Dilution and control

Dilution is a reduction in an existing owner's percentage when new shares are issued. Control concerns who can make which decisions; it depends on voting rights and agreements as well as percentages.

For example

Maya may own a smaller percentage after raising money while still leading the company. A minority investor may nevertheless have approval rights over particular major decisions.

Why it matters

The price, the ownership percentage, and the decision rights all matter when comparing offers.

Valuation, an exit, and a distribution

A valuation estimates what an investment is worth. An exit means selling some or all of it, for example to another company or investor. A distribution transfers money or other proceeds from the fund to its investors.

For example

A report can show Maya's company gaining value without anyone receiving cash. A later sale may realize that value, a different value, or a loss.

Why it matters

An estimated gain is not spendable cash. Listing shares on a stock exchange also does not necessarily let every investor sell immediately.

05

A little history

These selected US and European milestones explain parts of today's industry. They are not a claim that business investment began at one moment or followed the same path everywhere.

  1. 1946: organizing investment in new ventures

    American Research and Development Corporation was established in the United States, with Georges Doriot as its president. It was one of the early modern venture investment organizations.

    What this helps explain

    It illustrates the move toward an organized combination of outside money, investment judgment, and support for new businesses.

    Source for 1946: organizing investment in new ventures

  2. The 1980s: the rise of debt-financed buyouts

    A major US buyout wave used substantial borrowing to acquire established companies. Research on this period examines both the financial structure and changes in ownership and management.

    What this helps explain

    This history helps explain why discussion of private equity often centers on control, company performance, and the pressure created by debt.

    Source for The 1980s: the rise of debt-financed buyouts

  3. 1994 onward: public institutions support European finance

    The European Investment Fund was established in 1994. Its role includes working with financial partners to improve businesses' access to finance, including investment capital.

    What this helps explain

    Private business investment can involve public institutions alongside private investors. Different participants may combine commercial return goals with broader economic aims.

    Source for 1994 onward: public institutions support European finance

06

Follow one complete example

Maya's software business: raising money or selling ownership

Maya and her company, ShiftPlan, are fictional. Follow a young-company investment, then compare it with an alternative buyout situation. The euro amounts teach the mechanics; they are not market prices or return forecasts. The ownership arithmetic assumes all shares have the same rights, and nobody else holds shares or rights to obtain them.

  1. Investors choose a fund and its purpose

    Before investing in ShiftPlan, a manager raises a fund with an agreed strategy: what kinds of companies it may back and how it will operate. Investors examine the team, terms, and track record before committing money.

    Why this step matters

    The manager is investing other people's money within an agreement. A venture strategy and a buyout strategy may belong in different funds.

  2. Maya and the investor examine each other

    Maya explains what the extra money would help achieve. The venture investor checks customers, finances, technology, and the team. This investigation is called due diligence. Maya also asks other founders what the investor is like when a company struggles.

    Why this step matters

    Both sides are choosing a relationship that may last years.

    When things go differently

    A promising sales forecast may depend heavily on one customer renewing. The parties need to understand that dependency before treating the forecast as dependable.

  3. Agree on the price and rights

    They agree to value the existing ownership at €8 million before the investment. The fund will put €2 million into newly issued shares. In this simplified case, the ownership value immediately after investment is €10 million: the fund owns 20% and Maya owns 80%.

    Why this step matters

    Maya now owns a smaller percentage of a business with more resources. Separately, the agreement determines board seats and approval rights. A board oversees major company decisions.

  4. Complete the investment and follow the cash

    Required approvals and documents are completed, and the fund arranges its cash, calling investor commitments as needed. The €2 million goes into ShiftPlan's bank account because this is a new-share investment. Maya receives no personal sale proceeds in this version.

    Why this step matters

    Fundraising by a fund, investment into a company, and payment to a founder are separate transactions.

    When things go differently

    Compare an alternative mature-business case: a buyout fund pays Maya €8 million for 80% of her existing shares. That payment goes to Maya, and ShiftPlan receives none of it. Extra company funding would need a separate arrangement.

  5. Work on the business and face setbacks

    ShiftPlan hires carefully and tests a new sales approach. Maya's team still runs the company; investors may help with recruitment, introductions, and major decisions. Management reports progress and cash needs.

    Why this step matters

    The investment agreement starts the ownership period; it does not deliver business improvement by itself.

    When things go differently

    Sales take longer than expected. The team revises spending and milestones. The fund considers whether further investment is justified; another round of funding is not automatic.

  6. Sell an investment and return proceeds

    Imagine a successful later sale of all ShiftPlan's shares for €15 million. If the fund still owns 20%, rights are equal, and no other claims or costs intervene, its share is €3 million. Against its €2 million investment, that is a €1 million gain before costs. The fund then handles proceeds under its agreement, including any distributions.

    Why this step matters

    The company sale price, the fund's proceeds, its gain, and the investors' final receipts are different amounts.

    When things go differently

    The sale could happen later, at a lower price, or never. Later funding could also change the ownership percentages or payment rights. If the business fails with nothing left for shareholders, the equity investment can be lost.

The outcome

The venture case gave ShiftPlan resources to grow; the buyout comparison gave an existing owner a way to sell. The investor's result depends on the price paid, business performance, ownership terms, costs, and eventual proceeds. A signed deal is only the beginning.

07

Value, money, and incentives

Business improvement and investor returns are related, but different

Better products, stronger customer relationships, or more effective operations may make a company more valuable. Investors' returns also depend on the purchase price, borrowing, and what a future buyer will pay. Growth alone does not settle the calculation.

For example

ShiftPlan can win more customers yet disappoint its investors if the initial price assumed much faster growth. Conversely, a financial gain does not by itself prove better outcomes for employees or customers.

The manager is paid separately from the companies it owns

A management fee pays for investment management. Carried interest, often called carry, is an agreed share of investment profits. Eligibility, calculation, and payment order depend on the fund agreement. Neither is simply the revenue of a portfolio company.

For example

ShiftPlan earns revenue by selling software. A fund may later earn a gain by selling its shares. The manager's fee and possible profit share are another layer, and they affect what fund investors ultimately receive.

Useful success measures follow the whole journey

At company level, ask whether customers stay, the business generates or consumes cash, and its plans are working. At fund level, distinguish cash already returned from the estimated value of unsold holdings, and account for time and costs.

For example

A fund reporting a large estimated gain is in a different position from one that has already returned the same amount in cash. Neither number alone tells the whole story.

08

What makes this domain difficult

Choosing under uncertainty

Investors must decide before they know how a company will perform. Founders usually know more about their business, while investors may see patterns across several businesses.

The competing needs

Waiting for more certainty can mean missing an opportunity; acting quickly can mean buying an attractive story with weak foundations.

In practice

ShiftPlan's enthusiastic early customers may not represent a much larger market.

How people respond, and its limits

Check underlying assumptions, speak with customers, and identify what would change the decision. Investigation reduces avoidable mistakes; it cannot remove uncertainty.

Growing while keeping enough cash

Hiring and product development use money before their benefits arrive. Young businesses may need another investment before they can fund themselves.

The competing needs

Spending faster can accelerate learning and growth, but leaves less time to recover from setbacks.

In practice

ShiftPlan hires its full planned sales team before discovering which sales approach works.

How people respond, and its limits

Stage spending against meaningful progress and revisit how long available cash will last. Reserve money for follow-on investments where appropriate, while recognizing that neither investors nor companies have unlimited resources.

Using debt without making the business fragile

A buyout financed partly with borrowing creates repayment and interest obligations. Weaker trading or more expensive borrowing can reduce the room to maneuver.

The competing needs

Borrowing reduces the initial ownership money needed, but can amplify losses and compete with spending on people and products.

In practice

An established ShiftPlan loses a major customer while acquisition-related loan payments still fall due.

How people respond, and its limits

Examine less favorable operating scenarios and preserve financial room for them. More conservative borrowing can help, but no financing structure makes a weak business automatically strong.

Agreeing whose priorities shape the company

Founders, managers, fund investors, employees, and customers can want different things at different times. Ownership brings influence, but the consequences are shared more widely.

The competing needs

Closer oversight can improve discipline or undermine useful autonomy. Cost reductions can improve short-term figures while damaging service or employee trust.

In practice

Cutting support staff improves ShiftPlan's immediate costs, but slower responses cause customers to leave later.

How people respond, and its limits

Make decision rights clear, test the operating consequences of changes, and discuss conflicts openly. Aligned incentives and transparent reporting help; they do not eliminate disagreement.

Turning years of work into an actual return

A good business may have no attractive buyer when the fund wants to sell. Many funds have an agreed lifetime, but the companies and markets follow their own schedules.

The competing needs

Selling now provides money sooner; waiting may improve the price or make the outcome worse.

In practice

ShiftPlan meets its operating goals just as potential buyers reduce their acquisition budgets.

How people respond, and its limits

Prepare credible sale information early, consider more than one route, and communicate uncertainty about timing. Report unsold investments as estimates, without presenting them as completed cash returns.

09

Common misunderstandings

VC and PE are two completely separate kinds of money.

A better way to understand it

Venture capital belongs to the wider private-equity family. The everyday VC-versus-PE comparison usually means young-company investment versus buyouts, with growth investment in between.

An investment announcement tells us how much money the company can spend.

A better way to understand it

Some money may buy shares from existing owners. Follow the payment to distinguish company funding from seller proceeds.

A larger valuation means investors have already made that money.

A better way to understand it

The valuation is an estimate. Investors still need proceeds, and their receipts depend on ownership, obligations, costs, and the fund agreement.

Every good business should seek venture capital.

A better way to understand it

A healthy business can grow using customer income or other financing. Venture investing usually needs the possibility of substantial growth and an eventual investor return; that is not every owner's aim.

10

The ideas to take with you

  • Start with the business need: funding a young company, expanding an established one, or changing its ownership.

  • Keep the firm, fund, company, and individual owners separate when following money.

  • Price, ownership percentage, and decision rights tell different parts of an investment's story.

  • Business progress matters, but investor results also depend on costs, debt, sale conditions, and time.

  • An estimated value is not a distribution, and a completed investment is not a guaranteed success.

11

Try it for yourself

Pause and think through each situation, then open the answer.

1. A fund pays Maya €8 million for shares she already owns. How much of that payment can ShiftPlan use to hire people?

Reveal answer to question 1

None automatically. The payment goes to Maya as the seller.

The reasoning

The company needs its own funding arrangement to receive cash. Buying shares from an owner and paying the company for new shares have different recipients.

2. An investor commits €10 million and has so far paid €2 million into the fund. Has the fund already received the full commitment?

Reveal answer to question 2

No. It has received €2 million of that investor's commitment.

The reasoning

The remaining commitment is a future funding obligation under the agreement, not cash already in the fund's bank account.

3. ShiftPlan wins more customers, but its fund cannot find a buyer. Have those gains become cash returns for investors?

Reveal answer to question 3

Not simply because the business improved or its estimated value rose.

The reasoning

Business progress may support future value. Turning ownership into proceeds, and distributing them, are additional steps with their own uncertainties.

4. Why might Maya reject the investor offering the highest headline valuation?

Reveal answer to question 4

Its decision rights, expectations, or approach to setbacks might make it a poor partner.

The reasoning

The relationship extends beyond the price. Ownership terms and practical support can affect how the business develops for years.

12

Questions to ask a practitioner

  • What business need does this investment serve, and why is this fund suited to it?

  • Which money goes into the company, which goes to sellers, and what obligations accompany it?

  • Which decisions change hands after investment, and which remain with management?

  • What would make the investment case fail, and who would feel the effects?

  • How much reported value has actually been returned, and what remains uncertain?
