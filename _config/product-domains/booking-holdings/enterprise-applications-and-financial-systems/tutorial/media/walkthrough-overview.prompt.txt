Use case: scientific-educational
Asset type: one illustration in a plain-language product-domain tutorial for adults.
Domain: Following the money: how a travel company's financial systems work
Reader: A curious adult from a different field who has never worked in accounting, payments or enterprise software. No knowledge of bookkeeping, SAP or software architecture is assumed.
Scope: An introduction to the financial systems behind a large online travel company: how a booking turns into payments, payouts, revenue, taxes and published financial statements, and why the software that does this needs deliberate design. Booking Holdings (Booking.com, Priceline, Agoda, KAYAK, OpenTable) is the running context, and facts about it come from its public filings and job postings. The guesthouse booking, the people and the amounts in the walkthrough are fictional. Traveler-facing apps, marketing and human-resources systems are outside this guide. Accounting and tax rules differ by country; the explanation describes general patterns for a United States-listed company that operates heavily in Europe.
Subject: Lena books three nights at Casa Azul in Lisbon
Setup: This is a fictional teaching case with illustrative amounts and simplified timing. Lena, who lives in Germany, books three nights at Casa Azul, a small guesthouse in Lisbon, through a large online travel platform. The booking is on the merchant model: Lena pays the platform when she books, in March, for a stay in July. The total is 300 euros, and the platform's share is 15 percent, or 45 euros. Joana runs Casa Azul. Priya is a controller in the platform's finance team. Tom is an enterprise architect.
Step 1: Lena books and pays. Lena pays 300 euros by card. The payment service provider authorizes the payment. The booking system sends a booking event to finance: who booked, which property, which legal entity of the platform, the amount, the currency and the stay dates. Why: From this moment the platform holds money that belongs, for now, partly to Lena and mostly to Casa Azul. The event is the raw material for every later financial record, so it must be complete and mean the same thing in every brand. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 2: The money arrives, but not as one payment. Three days later the provider deposits one large amount covering many thousands of payments, minus its fees, together with a settlement file. Finance systems match Lena's 300 euros in the file to her booking and match the deposit to the bank statement. The 300 euros is recorded as cash held and as an amount owed (deferred merchant bookings), not as income. Why: This three-way match of booking, provider file and bank is reconciliation. If it fails at scale, nobody can say with certainty how much travelers' money the company holds. Possible complication: If a new local payment method is launched before its settlement files can be matched automatically, its payments pile up as unexplained items that people must investigate by hand.
Step 3: Lena shortens her stay. In May, Lena changes her booking to two nights. Under the property's policy she receives 100 euros back. The refund is sent through the provider, the amount held for the booking drops to 200 euros, and the expected payout to Casa Azul and the platform's future share are recalculated. Why: This is the exception that makes the work hard. Every record built so far must be adjusted consistently, in the payment records, the subledger and the expected payout, or the books will no longer agree with the money. Possible complication: If the refund is processed in the payment system but the booking change reaches finance late, the subledger briefly shows the old amount. Reconciliation will flag the difference, and someone must explain it before the month closes.
Step 4: Lena stays and revenue is recognized. Lena stays in July. The platform has now delivered what it promised, so its share of the reduced booking, 30 euros, becomes revenue. The remaining 170 euros becomes a payable to Casa Azul. Tax systems work out which taxes apply to the platform's fee and in which country. Why: Cash arrived in March, but income is recorded in July. The rules connect the financial record to the real service, not to the moment money moved. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 5: Joana is paid. The payout system groups all of Casa Azul's completed stays, applies deductions, and sends Joana 170 euros for Lena's stay by bank transfer, with a statement explaining the amount. Why: For Joana this payout is what matters most. A late or unexplained payment damages trust and creates calls to support. For the platform, the payout clears the amount it owed and must be matched to the bank once again. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 6: The month closes and the quarter is reported. At the end of July, the subledger sends summarized entries to the general ledger. Priya's team reconciles accounts, reviews unexplained differences, and confirms that controls ran, for example that no one changed payout bank details without a second approval. At quarter end, the results of all brands and legal entities are combined (consolidated) into the published statements. Why: Lena's booking is now part of one line in a report read by investors and auditors. Auditors can pick that line and ask to see the evidence all the way back to her booking. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 7: An architecture question appears. Priya notices that another brand in the group records partial refunds differently, so its numbers need manual adjustment every month. She raises it with Tom. Tom brings it to the architecture review: should every brand send refund events in the shared format to the central subledger, and by when? The options are compared on cost, risk and benefit, the decision is recorded, and conformance is tracked. Why: One-off workarounds are cheap once and expensive forever. A recorded decision and a shared standard turn a recurring manual fix into a design that scales, without weakening controls. Possible complication: None specified.
Outcome: Lena got a correct refund, Joana was paid the right amount on time, the platform earned its fee when the stay happened, and the published numbers could be traced back to individual bookings. Success here means that money, records and promises stayed in step through a change of plans, across systems built by different teams.

Teaching scene:
A left-to-right comic strip in five panels with the same characters throughout. 1: Lena (traveler) pays on a phone, label 'March: pays 300'. 2: a bank building receives one bundle from a payment company, label 'Settlement, minus fees'. 3: Lena changes her plans and gets money back, label 'Exception: 100 refunded'. 4: Joana (guesthouse owner) receives money, label 'July: payout 170'. 5: Priya (finance) ticks a checklist next to a report, label 'Month-end close'. Only these labels; friendly and clear. Do not draw any real company logos or brand marks; use generic icons only. The payment company is a plain generic building icon labeled 'Payment provider'.

Style and composition:
- Aspect ratio 16:9, generous margins, bright warm-white background.
- A polished adult educational illustration: flat editorial drawing, crisp navy outlines,
  restrained teal, blue, and warm amber accents, subtle texture, very little shading.
- Make the concept understandable at the width of a reading column and on a phone.
- Use a small number of recognizable objects and people. Keep relationships spatially clear.
- Use an inclusive cast without occupational or cultural stereotypes.
- Short everyday labels are welcome where they teach the distinction. Use only labels
  explicitly requested in the teaching scene; otherwise keep the image free of text.
- Arrows must have a clear meaning and direction; do not connect unrelated comparison
  panels as though they are required stages. Do not imply certainty, causality, proportions,
  cash movements, or guaranteed outcomes that the explanation does not support.
- Keep all important content inside the frame. No headline, watermark, brand logo,
  decorative charts, invented statistics, jargon, tiny writing, photorealism, or 3D rendering.
- Explain the idea visually. The surrounding tutorial provides its full definition and caveats.
